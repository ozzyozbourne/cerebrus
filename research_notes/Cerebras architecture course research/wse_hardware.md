## 1. Headline specs: WSE-1 vs WSE-2 vs WSE-3 (and WSE-3T / CS-4, 2026)

### Takeaway
WSE-2 (TSMC 7nm, 2.6T transistors, 850k cores, 40 GB SRAM, 20 PB/s memory BW, 220 Pb/s fabric) and WSE-3 (TSMC 5nm, 4T transistors, 900k cores, 44 GB SRAM, 21 PB/s, 214 Pb/s fabric, 125 PF "peak AI") are both full-wafer dies of about 46,225 mm². The per-core SRAM stays at 48 KB. The main per-core gain in WSE-3 is that the FP16 SIMD doubled from 4-wide to 8-wide. In Aug 2026 Cerebras announced WSE-3T ("Turbo") and the rack-scale CS-4.

### Cited Findings
**WSE-1 (CS-1, 2019; for context)**
- 1.2 trillion transistors, 400,000 cores, 46,225 mm² (vendor) — [Cerebras WSE-2 press release](https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-systems-smashes-the-2-5-trillion-transistor-mark-with-new-second-generation-wafer-scale-engine) (via search extract)
- CS-1: about 380,000 usable tiles, each with 48 KB local SRAM, about 18 GB on-wafer in total. A BiCGStab stencil solve reached about 0.86 PFLOPS, roughly one third of peak (Cerebras/NETL authors, measured on hardware) — [Rocki et al., SC20, arXiv:2010.03660](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03660)

**WSE-2 (CS-2, 2021)**

- Peak FP16: a published-peak table lists WSE-2 at 75 PF FP16 (sparse). Applying the 10:1 sparsity ratio analysts assumed for the CS-2 gives about 7.5 PF dense (analyst inference) — [SemiAnalysis "Faster Tokens Please"](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/cerebras-faster-tokens-please); [Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/cerebras-cs-3-bring-on-the-nvidia-blackwell-competition/)

(This is the SDK's own assumption, not an official spec sheet, and the files do not say which generation it applies to.)

Vendor claims "2x the speed" of CS-3 and "up to 10x throughput per watt" — [Cerebras investor release](https://investors.cerebras.ai/news-releases/news-release-details/cerebras-unveils-cs-4-30-times-faster-gpu-based-solutions); [STH power delivery page](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-talks-going-rack-scale-with-their-wses-at-hot-chips-2026/cerebras-cs-4-hot-chips-2026-power-delivery-continued/); [linuxpro summary](https://www.linuxpro.com.br/en/2026/09/cerebras-cs-4-inferencia-ia-video/)

### Inferences
- 44 GB / 900k cores ≈ 48.9 KB per core and 40 GB / 850k ≈ 47 KB per core. Both are consistent with 48 KB per PE, with "GB" being loose decimal/binary marketing rounding.
- 21 PB/s ÷ 900k ≈ 23 GB/s per core. At about 1 GHz-class clocks that is roughly 3 × 8-byte or 6 × 32-bit accesses per cycle per core, consistent with 8 single-cycle 32-bit banks (see §3). This is my derivation, not a vendor statement.
- WSE-2→WSE-3 gives about 1.06× more cores and 2× FP16 SIMD width (4→8). That accounts for most of the claimed 2× per-system performance at similar power.
- 750×994 = 745,500 physical fabric positions on CS-2, vs the "850,000 cores" marketing figure. The gap is consistent with spare or redundant cores and edge I/O not being exposed to SDK users. This is an inference; Cerebras has not published the breakdown. Some arXiv papers similarly say "around 750,000 PEs" for CS-2 — [Wafer-scale FFT, arXiv:2209.15040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15040) (via search extract).

### Gaps
- No official clock frequency for WSE-2 or WSE-3 was found in any source. The only number is the SDK's 850 MHz assumption.
- The WSE-3 user-visible fabric dimensions (the equivalent of 750×994) were not found locally or via search.
- No primary source pins down whether 15 kW is chip power and about 20 kW system power for CS-2.

## 2. Wafer-scale integration: yield, defects, cross-reticle wiring, packaging, cooling, power

### Takeaway
Cerebras prints a normal array of reticle-sized "dies" on one 300 mm wafer. It stitches them with on-wafer wires across the scribe lines, so the fabric is uniform across the whole wafer. It tolerates defects by making cores tiny, holding spare cores in reserve, and using reconfigurable fabric links to route around bad ones. Power is delivered into the face of the wafer, and a closed water loop handles cooling ("engine block").

### Cited Findings
- **Redundancy mechanism.** HC31 (2019) lists "redundant cores" plus "redundant fabric links". The fabric is reconfigured to route around defective cores using spare cores (vendor) — [HC31 Sean Lie slides](https://old.hotchips.org/hc31/HC31_1.13_Cerebras.SeanLie.v02.pdf)
- The yield blog says a routing architecture reconfigures connections between cores, with a small reserve of spare cores, and "achieves high yield with minimal spare capacity". The fabric "automatically routes around [a defect] using redundant communication pathways" (vendor, Jan 2025) — [Cerebras blog "100x Defect Tolerance"](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/100x-defect-tolerance-how-cerebras-solved-the-yield-problem); [Cerebras on X](https://x.com/cerebras/status/1878926975571763571)
- **Small-core argument.** Total chip area is about 50× a GPU while core size is about 100× smaller, so the WSE is "~100x more fault tolerant than a GPU when considering the silicon area affected by each defect". With 0.001 defects/mm², the WSE "sees 46 defects and loses just 2.2mm²" of silicon, a "164x reduction in dead silicon area" (vendor modeling; 100× and 164× use different assumptions). The WSE-3 core is about 0.05 mm², roughly 1/100 of an H100 SM — [Cerebras blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/100x-defect-tolerance-how-cerebras-solved-the-yield-problem); [arXiv:2503.11698 comparison paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.11698)
- An external comparison paper notes the wafer area is not all cores (about 50% is SRAM, register files, and fabric) and repeats that the configurable fabric recovers "almost full yield" — [arXiv:2503.11698](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.11698)
- The HC34 slides give a 50:50 logic-to-SRAM area ratio per core — [HC34 Cerebras slides](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf)
- A secondary claim that each wafer has a unique pattern of about 70,000 disabled cores comes from Medium and is **unverified** — [Medium article](https://machine-learning-made-simple.medium.com/why-cerebras-raised-at-56-4-billion-usd-to-attack-nvidias-memory-bottleneck-c11bfe81f417)
- An academic comparison (Theseus) notes that the "known-good-die" approach (e.g., Tesla Dojo) may yield higher than Cerebras's die-stitching approach. That is a counterpoint, not a measurement — [Theseus, arXiv:2407.02079](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.02079)
- Wires between cores are under 1 mm, which lowers data-movement energy. The fabric is uniform across the whole wafer, including across die boundaries — [ServeTheHome HC34](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-wafer-scale-engine-wse-2-and-cs-2-at-hot-chips-34/)
- Secondary summaries list the key problems and fixes: reticle limits (handled by reticle stitching), defects (redundant cores), power delivery (vertical delivery into the wafer face), cooling (liquid), and thermal expansion (a custom connector that lets the wafer move without cracking) — [Medium](https://machine-learning-made-simple.medium.com/why-cerebras-raised-at-56-4-billion-usd-to-attack-nvidias-memory-bottleneck-c11bfe81f417); [gpusmith](https://gpusmith.com/articles/en/cerebras-wafer-scale-engine-explained). (Secondary sources. The HC31 slides are the primary source for the stitching, connector, and power details, but I could not open them.)
- CS-3 "engine block" packaging: power delivered into the face of the wafer, uniform cooling from a proprietary closed internal water loop — [gpusmith](https://gpusmith.com/articles/en/cerebras-wafer-scale-engine-explained) (secondary)
- CS-4 (2026): AC/DC converters about 0.5 mm from the wafer, removing the PCB from the final power path — [STH HC2026](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-talks-going-rack-scale-with-their-wses-at-hot-chips-2026/cerebras-cs-4-hot-chips-2026-power-delivery-continued/)

### Inferences
- Because the repair happens in the fabric, software (the SDK) sees a clean logical rectangle (e.g., 750×994 on CS-2) rather than a wafer with holes. That explains why marketing core counts (850k) exceed the SDK-visible grid.

### Gaps
- I found no primary numbers for spare-core ratio (e.g., one spare column per N), the exact cross-reticle wire pitch or count, the voltage/current per wafer, or coolant flow rates. The HC31 2019 slides (primary) cover stitching and packaging but could not be opened.

## 3. Processing element internals (CE, SRAM, registers, SIMD, sparsity, microthreads, ISA)

### Takeaway
Each PE is a small core plus a router. The core has 48 KB SRAM (8 single-ported 6 KB banks, single-cycle, interleaved by 32-bit word) shared by code and data, plus a tiny software-managed cache (256 B on WSE-2, 512 B on WSE-3). It also has GPRs and "data structure registers" that hold DSDs, and a SIMD datapath: 4-wide FP16 on WSE-1/2, 8-wide FP16/BF16 and 16-wide INT8 on WSE-3. Instructions are triggered by dataflow: arriving fabric data selects the handler, and zeros are filtered at the sender, so sparse work is skipped. Up to 8 concurrent tensor operations ("microthreads") run per core.

### Cited Findings
- **SRAM, WSE-2 (HC34).** "48kB total memory per PE" in 8 single-port banks, plus a "256B software-managed cache" for high-frequency data such as accumulators. Memory is all SRAM with single-cycle access — [HC34 slides](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf)
- **SRAM, WSE-3 (HC2024).** 48 kB SRAM per core plus 512 B local cache — [HC2024 slides](https://hc2024.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/72_HC2024.Cerebras.Sean.v03.final.pdf). So 48 KB is **unchanged WSE-1→2→3**, and the cache doubled from 256 B to 512 B.
- Memory organization (academic): 8 banks × 6 KB, each 32 bits wide, with successive 32-bit words in successive banks. Code and data share the space, and each PE's 48 KB is private to it — [arXiv:2605.07954 Stencils on WSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07954); [arXiv:2209.15040 FFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15040) (via search extract)
- **[SDK 2.10.1]** Tutorial code comments say "48 kB of global memory contain A, x, b, y". `spmv-hypersparse/run.py` refers to the "memory capacity (48KB) of the PE" but asserts per-PE use stays under 46 KB. The bandwidth test buffers PEs with a "46KB fifo", implying about 2 KB is reserved for code/runtime in that configuration — `tutorials/gemv-02-memory-dsds/pe_program.csl`, `benchmarks/spmv-hypersparse/run.py`, `benchmarks/bandwidth-test/run.py`
- **WSE-3 core (HC2024).** 16 general-purpose registers, 48 data structure registers (which hold DSDs), 8-way 16-bit SIMD for FP16/BF16, 16-way 8-bit SIMD for fixed-point/INT8 (vendor) — [HC2024 slides](https://hc2024.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/72_HC2024.Cerebras.Sean.v03.final.pdf) (via search extract)
- **WSE-1/2 SIMD.** A secondary analysis says WSE-1 and WSE-2 used a 4-wide FP16 SIMD, which WSE-3 doubled to 8-wide — [hyper-accel blog](https://hyper-accel.github.io/en/posts/cerebras-wse/) (secondary; I did not confirm a 4-wide figure in the HC34 extract)
- **Dataflow triggering and sparsity.** The HC34 talk describes "native sparsity acceleration" through dataflow scheduling: incoming fabric data triggers a lookup of the handler instructions, and zeros are filtered out so no compute is spent on them — [HC34 slides](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf); [Cerebras architecture deep dive blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning). The MICRO-54 keynote describes the sender filtering out zero data — [Lie MICRO-54 keynote](https://microarch.org/micro54/media/lie-keynote.pdf). "If a weight is zero, no wavelet is sent" — [wafer.substack](https://wafer.substack.com/p/breaking-down-the-cerebras-wafer) (secondary)
- **Microthreads.** "The core also supports eight simultaneous tensor operations, which we call micro-threads" (WSE-2, vendor) — [Cerebras architecture deep dive](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning). A secondary source says the WSE-3 scheduler switches among 8 microthreads every cycle based on operand availability — [hyper-accel blog](https://hyper-accel.github.io/en/posts/cerebras-wse/)
- **[SDK 2.10.1] WSE-2 vs WSE-3 microthreads.** "On WSE-2, the queue ID of an input or output fabric DSD corresponds to the ID of the microthread in which that operation executes. On WSE-3, queue IDs and microthreads can be decoupled, so that any microthread ID 0 to 7 can be used with any of queues 0 to 7." "Unlike WSE-2, the WSE-3 architecture exposes microthread IDs." "Two operations cannot concurrently use the same microthread." A FIFO "requires two microthreads and a scratch buffer" — `tutorials/topic-15-wse3-microthreads/README.rst`, `tutorials/topic-09-fifos/README.rst`
- **Instruction concepts.** DSD operations such as `@fmacs` (FMA scalar × vector), `@fadds`, and `@fmovs` operate on whole tensors described by DSDs. They run asynchronously on a microthread, and on completion they can activate a task — **[SDK 2.10.1]** `tutorials/gemv-02-memory-dsds/README.rst`, `tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1]** Sparse wavelets: "Since wavelet payloads are 32 bits wide, we can use the lower 16 bits to contain data … [and] the upper 16 bits to contain the index of the value." A task can take two 16-bit arguments or one u32 — `tutorials/topic-04-sparse-tensors/README.rst`

### Inferences
- The FLOPs per core per cycle implied for WSE-3 are 8 FP16 FMAs = 16 FLOPs/cycle. 900k × 16 × ~1 GHz ≈ 14–15 PF dense. That matches the analyst's ~15.6 PF dense estimate, which supports the view that 125 PF is a sparse/marketing number. This is my arithmetic; the clock is unconfirmed.

### Gaps
- WSE-2 GPR/DSR counts and the exact FP32 SIMD width (probably 1-wide for fp32 FMAC, but no source) were not found.
- No public ISA encoding. Instruction memory and data share the 48 KB, and no separate I-cache was found.

## 4. Router and on-wafer fabric (mesh, ports, wavelets, colors, switches, filters)

### Takeaway
Each PE's router links to its 4 neighbors (N/S/E/W) and to its own CE via the "ramp", making 5 bidirectional ports. Traffic consists of 32-bit wavelets tagged with a color, a virtual channel ID with 24 routable colors (IDs 0–23) on WSE-2. The color selects a statically configured route, which can multicast. A wavelet moves one hop per cycle; the ramp takes about 2 cycles. Routes are set at compile time; at runtime, control wavelets can advance "switch" positions, and "filters" can drop wavelets selectively.

### Cited Findings
- 5-port router: "to 4 neighbors and core" — [HC34 slides](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf). "The router manages 5 bidirectional links: 4 between the neighboring routers and a ramp link connected to the processor" — [arXiv:2404.15888 Near-Optimal Wafer-Scale Reduce](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15888)
- **Link and latency.** "The bandwidth of a link is a 32-bit word per clock, and the latency of a hop is one clock cycle on average" — [arXiv:2209.15040 FFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15040). The ramp link (router↔processor) has about 2 cycles of latency vs 1 cycle between routers — [arXiv:2605.07954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07954). A wavelet crosses any link in 1 cycle, but it takes T_R cycles from entering the router until an instruction can be issued on it — [arXiv:2404.15888](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15888). WSE-3: "a 32-bit message … can be sent between neighboring PEs in a single clock cycle in each direction" — [arXiv:2604.27985 SpMM on CS-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27985)
- **Colors.** The SDK docs say each color has an ID from 0 to 23, and each wavelet carries a 5-bit color tag. The color determines both the route and which task (if any) consumes the wavelet — [SDK docs "Wafer-Scale Engine Architecture"](https://sdk.cerebras.ai/computing-with-cerebras) (via search extract). "Each router supports … 24 per PE and an additional 8 reserved" — [SpaDA arXiv:2511.09447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09447). "24 local static routes … called colors"; multicast is allowed; two same-colored wavelets arriving in the same cycle is undefined behavior — [arXiv:2605.07954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07954)
- **[SDK 2.10.1]** The color config registers are memory-mapped at `0x7f40–0x7f57` ("colors 0-23"), i.e., 24 entries (found in a CSL source comment in the examples).
- Deadlock and lossless operation: "dedicated input buffers for each color at each router … no wavelet is dropped and … communication remains deadlock-free" — [arXiv:2209.15040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15040)
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Static route configuration.** In `layout.csl`, `@set_color_config` sets per-PE, per-color rx/tx directions. Example: on the left PE, color 0 receives from RAMP and transmits EAST; on the right PE, it receives from WEST and transmits down the RAMP — `tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst`. `SdkLayout` can allocate physical colors from symbolic ones and auto-route between code regions — `tutorials/sdklayout-01-introduction/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Switches.** "Fabric switches permit limited runtime control of routes." Besides the default route (position 0), positions 1, 2, and 3 can be defined. The router moves to a new position when it receives a control wavelet. Switches can save colors (the example uses 1 color instead of 4). The new position takes effect only after the control wavelet itself has passed, so that wavelet's payload goes to the PE on the old route — `tutorials/topic-06-switches/README.rst`, `tutorials/topic-07-switches-entrypt/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Filters.** "Fabric filters allow a PE to selectively accept incoming wavelets." Range filters act on the upper 16 bits of the wavelet; in the example each recipient processes a quarter of a broadcast stream — `tutorials/topic-08-filters/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Color swap (WSE-2 only).** With `swap_color_x`, a wavelet arriving from WEST leaves EAST with the lowest color bit flipped (red 0 ↔ blue 1), which supports alternating patterns. "CSL currently does not support color swap on WSE-3" — `tutorials/topic-14-color-swap/README.rst`
- Aggregate fabric bandwidth: WSE-2 220 Pb/s; WSE-3 214 Pb/s; WSE-3T 53.5 PB/s (≈428 Pb/s) (vendor) — see §1 sources

### Inferences
- 5 color bits give 32 IDs. 24 are routable and the other 8 are reserved or used as non-routable control task IDs, which matches the SpaDA "24 + 8 reserved" description and SDK task IDs going up to 30 (see §5).
- Per-link 32 bits/cycle × 4 directions × 2 (bidirectional) × ~850k PEs × ~1 GHz gives on the order of 2×10^17 b/s, consistent with the ~214–220 Pb/s vendor numbers. My arithmetic, assuming a ~1 GHz clock.

### Gaps
- Router buffer depths per color and the exact WSE-3 routable color count were not found. On WSE-3, routable data reception goes through 8 input queues (see §5), but the number of route-table colors on WSE-3 is not confirmed here.

## 5. Tasks and the task scheduler

### Takeaway
There is no OS and no instruction stream in the usual sense. Code is a set of tasks, each bound to a task ID. A data task (wavelet-triggered task) runs when a wavelet of its color or queue arrives. A local task runs when software activates it. A control task runs when a control wavelet carrying its ID arrives. The hardware picks the next task from those that are both activated and unblocked. WSE-3 changes the ID space: data tasks bind to 8 input queues instead of 24 colors.

### Cited Findings
- "The hardware picks the next task to run from those that have been activated and unblocked." Wavelets on some colors do not activate a task and are consumed by a fabric DSD instead — [SDK docs](https://sdk.cerebras.ai/computing-with-cerebras) (via search extract)
- "Local tasks are activated programmatically and data tasks trigger automatically upon wavelet arrival"; "scheduling … via hardware-level microthreading" — [arXiv:2601.17754 MLIR stencils](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17754) (via search extract)
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Task ID ranges.** "On WSE-2, task IDs which can receive data wavelets are in the range 0 through 23, corresponding to the IDs of the colors. On WSE-3, task IDs which can receive data wavelets are in the range 0 through 7, corresponding to input queues which are bound to a routable color. … local tasks … on WSE-2 can be associated with any task ID from 0 to 30, and on WSE-3 … from 8 to 30." Non-routable control task IDs act as sentinels (end-of-stream markers) — `tutorials/topic-05-sentinels/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Data task flow.** When a wavelet of the bound color reaches the router, it is forwarded to the CE, which activates the task. The wavelet payload becomes the task argument. A PE can also trigger its own data task by sending wavelets to its router, which bounces them back down the ramp — `tutorials/topic-03-streaming-wavelet-data/README.rst`, `tutorials/topic-09-fifos/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Control wavelets.** A control wavelet can carry a task ID, which activates the bound control task on the receiving CE, with 16 bits of optional payload in the lower half. Control wavelets also advance switches — `tutorials/topic-07-switches-entrypt/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Async completion → task.** When an async `@fmovs`/`@fadds` finishes, it activates a local task (e.g., `exit_task`, which unblocks memcpy's command stream) — `tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] WSE-3 queue rules.** "On WSE-3, each output queue can only bind to a single color." To reuse a queue with another color, call `@queue_flush(oq)`. When the queue drains, the hardware triggers "special teardown task 29", which calls a registered handler (`@set_empty_queue_handler`) that rebinds the queue's color — `tutorials/topic-16-queue-flush/README.rst`, `tutorials/topic-15-wse3-microthreads/README.rst`

### Inferences
- A good diagram: fabric → router (color lookup) → ramp → input queue (WSE-3) or color (WSE-2) → task table → scheduler (activated ∧ unblocked) → handler / microthread.

### Gaps
- I found no primary detail on scheduler priority or arbitration among ready tasks, or the cycle cost of task switching.

## 6. Memory model: SRAM only, no caches/DRAM, DSDs

### Takeaway
The wafer has no DRAM and no hardware cache hierarchy. Each PE addresses only its own 48 KB SRAM, apart from a small software-managed cache. Everything else is reached by message passing over the fabric. DSDs (data structure descriptors) are hardware address generators held in DSRs. Memory DSDs describe affine (strided) walks over local SRAM; fabric DSDs (fabin/fabout) describe streams of wavelets on a color or queue. A single instruction such as `@fmacs` iterates over a whole DSD.

### Cited Findings
- Memory is all SRAM with single-cycle access — [HC34 slides](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf). WSE-3: 48 data structure registers per core — [HC2024 slides](https://hc2024.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/72_HC2024.Cerebras.Sean.v03.final.pdf)
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Memory DSD.** It specifies "how to loop over" an array: base address plus length, or a `tensor_access` expression with an induction variable, loop bound, and affine expression "to generate various addresses at runtime". Strided access (e.g., a column of a row-major matrix with stride N) and `increment_dsd_offset` are shown — `tutorials/gemv-02-memory-dsds/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1] Fabric DSDs.** `fabout_dsd` sends M wavelets along a color (and on WSE-3, an output queue). `fabin_dsd` receives from a color or input queue. Either can be an operand of DSD ops (e.g., `@fadds` with a fabin source accumulates incoming values directly from the fabric) — `tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst`, `tutorials/topic-15-wse3-microthreads/README.rst`
- A fabric output DSD specifies the wavelet's color and control bit. With a "control transform" it can emit control wavelets. On WSE-3 a fabric DSD is bound to a color indirectly through its queue, and DSD builtins allow at most one `fabin_dsd` operand — [SDK builtins](https://sdk.cerebras.ai/csl/language/builtins); [SDK release notes](https://sdk.cerebras.ai/sdk-release-notes/sdk-rel-notes-cumulative) (via search extract)
- **[SDK 2.10.1] FIFO DSDs** extend the "small hardware queues used for fabric communication" by buffering bursts in SRAM — `tutorials/topic-09-fifos/README.rst`
- A Cerebras patent describes DSDs as holding an operand's location, element count, and addressing — [US patent 11580394 "Accelerated deep learning"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11580394) (via search extract)

### Inferences
- The SDK matvec benchmark reports both "relative" bandwidth (what a cached architecture would need) and "absolute" bandwidth (actual SRAM accesses on WSE). This framing is useful for teaching that there is no cache to absorb reuse (**[SDK 2.10.1]** `benchmarks/single-tile-matvec/README.rst`).

### Gaps
- No DSR count for WSE-2. The full DSD format (dimensions, max length, bit widths) is not documented in the sources I could reach.

## 7. Host interface: getting data on and off the wafer

### Takeaway
The host talks to the CS-2/CS-3 over 100 GbE (12 × 100 GbE ≈ 1.2 Tb/s per system). Data enters the wafer through I/O channels at the fabric edge. The SDK's `memcpy` infrastructure claims a margin of PE columns and rows around the user rectangle to fan data in and out, in "copy" mode (write to a PE's SRAM) or "streaming" mode (deliver wavelets that trigger tasks).

### Cited Findings
- **[SDK 2.10.1]** "The host connects the device via 100Gbps ethernets. The data is distributed from/to couple of I/O channels. The maximum bandwidth of a single channel is around 7Gbps … the overhead of TCP is about 200 us." At most 16 I/O channels. Back-pressure from busy cores stalls I/O, so buffer columns (46 KB FIFO per PE) are used to overlap I/O with compute. The runtime aggregates nonblocking H2D/D2H commands to amortize TCP overhead — `benchmarks/bandwidth-test/run.py`, `benchmarks/bandwidth-test/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1]** memcpy modes: "copy" (all early GEMV tutorials) vs "streaming" (`MEMCPYH2D_DATA_1`/`_2`, `MEMCPYD2H_DATA_1` colors deliver data along the top row and left column, which trigger tasks). memcpy commands are serialized, so order matters (wrong order hangs). For memcpy streaming colors the user must not define routes, because the memcpy module routes them — `tutorials/gemv-09-streaming/README.rst`, `tutorials/topic-03-streaming-wavelet-data/README.rst`
- **[SDK 2.10.1]** The user rectangle must be ≤ fabric width − 7 and ≤ fabric height − 2 (memcpy/IO infrastructure). The CS-2 fabric is 750×994 — `benchmarks/single-tile-matvec/README.rst`
- CS-3 Ethernet "stays at 12x100GbE" (forum post citing Cerebras, less authoritative) — [SemiWiki forum](https://semiwiki.com/forum/threads/the-new-cerebras-cs-3.19821/)
- WSE-3T: 2.4 Tb/s off-wafer per wafer; CS-4 rack 7.2 Tb/s; RoCE v2 plus Direct Wafer Links (vendor) — [Cerebras investor release](https://investors.cerebras.ai/news-releases/news-release-details/cerebras-unveils-cs-4-30-times-faster-gpu-based-solutions)

### Inferences
- 16 channels × ~7 Gb/s ≈ 112 Gb/s, so an SDK program on a single host link is I/O-bound at about 14 GB/s. Filling 44 GB of SRAM this way would take about 3+ seconds, which shows why weights must stay resident or be streamed (§8).

### Gaps
- No source states CS-2 Ethernet as exactly 12×100 GbE (1.2 Tb/s); only the CS-3 forum post does. The physical location and count of the wafer-edge I/O and SerDes were not found.

## 8. System level: CS-2/CS-3, MemoryX, SwarmX, execution modes, Condor Galaxy, inference service

### Takeaway
For training, Cerebras moved from "layer pipelined" execution (the whole model resident in SRAM, layers mapped spatially; used on CS-1 and early CS-2) to "weight streaming" (2021+). In weight streaming, activations stay on the wafer, and weights live in MemoryX (DRAM+flash) and stream in one layer at a time through SwarmX. SwarmX broadcasts weights to many CS systems and sum-reduces gradients on the way back, and MemoryX applies the weight update. For inference (2024+), the reverse holds: weights stay resident in SRAM, pipelined across several wafers (e.g., Llama-70B's 80 layers over 4 CS-3s).

### Cited Findings
- **Weight streaming.** Parameters are stored off-chip in MemoryX and compute happens in the CS-2. The whole wafer computes one layer at a time — [Cerebras blog "Linear scaling with weight streaming"](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/linear-scaling-made-possible-with-weight-streaming); [Announcing the Cerebras Architecture for Extreme-Scale AI](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/announcing-the-cerebras-architecture-for-extreme-scale-ai)
- **MemoryX.** DRAM plus flash, computes weight updates. CS-2-era configurations ran from 4 TB to 2.4 PB, supporting 200B to 120T parameters (vendor) — [Cerebras brain-scale press release](https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-systems-announces-worlds-first-brain-scale-artificial-intelligence-solution). CS-3 era: "up to 1.2 petabytes" (vendor) — [Cerebras CS-3 blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-cs3)
- **SwarmX.** It "handles the broadcast of weights from MemoryX to the entire cluster and effectively reduces (sums) gradients in the opposite direction" — [Cerebras developer docs "How Cerebras works"](https://training-api.cerebras.ai/en/2.0.3/wsc/cerebras-basics/how-cerebras-works.html). Up to 192 CS-2s (CS-2 era) — [Cerebras press release](https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-systems-announces-worlds-first-brain-scale-artificial-intelligence-solution); up to 2,048 CS-3s, i.e., 256 EF (CS-3 era, vendor) — [Cerebras CS-3 blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-cs3); [Cerebras scale-out guide](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/the-complete-guide-to-scale-out-on-cerebras-wafer-scale-clusters)
- **Condor Galaxy (with G42).** CG-1 and CG-2 (California): each 64 CS-2, 4 EF, 82 TB. CG-3 (Dallas): 64 CS-3, 8 EF, 58M cores, 108 TB parameter memory, planned for Q2 2024 (vendor) — [Cerebras on X (CG-3)](https://x.com/CerebrasSystems/status/1767977185535734057); [Cerebras on X (108TB)](https://x.com/CerebrasSystems/status/1775188628811227389); [Cerebras AI Day deck](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/cerebras-ai-day-deck-a-closer-look-at-the-worlds-fastest-ai-chip/266911791)
- **Inference service (Aug 2024 launch).** Llama 3.1-70B at 450 tok/s (vendor). The 70B model needs about 140 GB at 16-bit, so its 80 layers are distributed across **four CS-3 systems connected over Ethernet** — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/08/27/cerebras_ai_inference/); [Cerebras blog "Introducing Cerebras Inference"](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/introducing-cerebras-inference-ai-at-instant-speed)
- Later: Llama 3.1-70B at 2,100 tok/s, "3x" faster (vendor, Oct 2024) — [Cerebras blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-inference-3x-faster). **Independent:** Artificial Analysis measured 2,314 tok/s on Llama 3.3 70B with 170 ms TTFT (reported in an interview; primary benchmark not checked) — [The Data Exchange](https://thedataexchange.media/cerebras-inference/)
- Rationale: "During inference, we don't need to access external memory to load parameters because they're already there and positioned near the compute cores" (vendor) — [The Data Exchange](https://thedataexchange.media/cerebras-inference/)
- Claims of 2,500 tok/s on 400B+ models appear only on low-reliability sites (**unverified**) — [SyncFlo](https://news.syncflo.xyz/2026-08-28-cerebras-systems-cs3-wafer-scale-engine-fast-inference.html)
- CS-4 (2026) is positioned for "ultrafast frontier inference": three wafers per rack with Direct Wafer Links — [Cerebras HC2026 blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/ultrafast-frontier-inference-cerebras-deep-dive-at-hot-chips-2026); [STH](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-talks-going-rack-scale-with-their-wses-at-hot-chips-2026/)

### Inferences
- 4 × 44 GB = 176 GB of SRAM for a 140 GB model leaves about 36 GB for KV cache and activations. That explains the four-wafer minimum for 70B at 16-bit. It also implies that larger models (e.g., 405B at 16-bit ≈ 810 GB) need ≥ 19 wafers, or lower precision. My arithmetic.
- Weight streaming and inference are inverse placements: training streams weights and keeps activations resident; inference keeps weights resident and streams tokens through layers.

### Gaps
- I did not find primary documentation of the original "layer pipelined" mode's mechanics on CS-1/CS-2 beyond the contrast with weight streaming. Current CG cluster status (late 2026) was not verified. Inference precision (16-bit vs 8-bit weights) per model was not confirmed.
