The Cerebras Wafer-Scale Engine is a single 46,225 mm² piece of silicon made from a whole 300 mm wafer. The WSE-2 (TSMC 7nm, 850,000 cores, 40 GB SRAM) and WSE-3 (TSMC 5nm, 900,000 cores, 44 GB SRAM) both place every byte of working memory next to compute: each processing element (PE) gets 48 KB of private, single-cycle SRAM, and there is no DRAM and no hardware cache anywhere on the wafer. PEs talk to their four neighbours by sending 32-bit "wavelets" tagged with one of 24 routable "colors". Each wavelet triggers a task on the PE that receives it, and on-chip "data structure descriptors" (DSDs) turn whole-vector loops into single instructions. Programmers write CSL, a Zig-like language, compiled with `cslc`. The program is a `layout.csl` that places code on a rectangle of PEs, plus per-PE programs, plus a Python host script built on `SdkRuntime` and the `memcpy` library, and all of it runs unchanged on a cycle-level simulator. At system scale, CS-2 and CS-3 training streams weights from external MemoryX through SwarmX ("weight streaming"), while the 2024+ inference service does the reverse and pins weights in SRAM across several wafers. The 2026 CS-4 packs three higher-clocked WSE-3T wafers into a rack. Compared with Tenstorrent (about 1.5 MB of SRAM per Tensix core, GDDR6, Ethernet meshes, open TT-Metalium) and NVIDIA (HBM, caches, SIMT CUDA), Cerebras trades memory capacity for extreme local bandwidth. The clearest independent evidence of the payoff is single-stream inference speed (981 tok/s on a ~1T-parameter model, 6.7× the fastest GPU cloud, as measured by Artificial Analysis) and strong-scaling HPC (179× Frontier on molecular dynamics that fits on one wafer). Its liabilities are capacity, power, cost per token at high batch, and a niche, low-level programming model.

## How to read the numbers in this report

Every figure carries a provenance tag, because vendor marketing, analyst inference and measured results disagree often enough to mislead a course. **[Vendor]** means a Cerebras, Tenstorrent or NVIDIA published or presented number. **[Indep]** means a third-party measurement. **[Analyst]** means a third-party estimate rather than a measurement. **[SDK 2.10.1]** means the fact was read directly from the installed Cerebras SDK 2.10.1 tutorials, benchmarks, standard library or tool output, which is the most reliable class here. **[Derived]** means arithmetic done by the researchers and not stated by any source. Generation labels (WSE-1, WSE-2, WSE-3, WSE-3T) are attached to every hardware number.

Two limits on the sources apply throughout. First, the research sessions could not reach `sdk.cerebras.ai`/`sdk.cerebras.net`, `cerebras.ai`, `docs.tenstorrent.com` or most NVIDIA pages directly. ## Chapter 1 — Decode needs about 1 FLOP per byte, and that is the whole argument

The case for wafer-scale starts with the roofline. An accelerator's ridge point is peak FLOP/s divided by memory bandwidth. For an H100 SXM running dense BF16, that is roughly **989 TFLOPS ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 295 FLOP/byte** [Vendor inputs, Derived ratio]. Kernels with lower arithmetic intensity are limited by bandwidth, not compute ([Spheron roofline](https://www.spheron.network/blog/the-roofline-model-for-llm-inference-are-you-compute-bound-o/)). LLM decode at batch 1 performs **about 1 FLOP per byte of weights read**. Batching raises intensity roughly in proportion to the number of concurrent sequences, but even batch 64 on a 70B model reaches only about 50–100 FLOP/byte, still under the ridge. Prefill, by contrast, can reach thousands of FLOP/byte and is compute-bound ([dev.to roofline](https://dev.to/ji_ai/why-llm-decoding-is-memory-bound-prefill-vs-decode-roofline-223f); [Zartis](https://www.zartis.com/you-dont-have-a-compute-problem-you-have-a-bytes-per-token-problem/)).

The memory wall is worsening by generation. From H100 to Rubin, HBM bandwidth grows about **5.7×** (3.35 → 19.2 TB/s) while low-precision FLOPs grow much faster because the number format also shrinks (BF16 → NVFP4) [Derived from Vendor figures]. Each architecture in this course answers that wall differently. GPUs add HBM bandwidth and rely on batching. Cerebras keeps weights in on-wafer SRAM: **WSE-3: 44 GB at 21 PB/s** [Vendor]. Tenstorrent splits the difference with about 1.5 MB of SRAM per core, cheap GDDR6 and Ethernet scale-out.

The opening chapter should flag one comparison trap. Cerebras markets "7,000×" the memory bandwidth and "880×" the on-chip memory of an H100 [Vendor] ([Cerebras chip page](https://cerebras.net/chip/), via search extract). That pits on-die SRAM against off-chip HBM. The 21 PB/s is aggregate *local* bandwidth spread across 900,000 PEs, each reaching only its own 48 KB, so a fairer GPU counterpart is aggregate register-file plus shared-memory bandwidth, which is also in the tens to hundreds of TB/s [Derived]. Visual idea: a roofline plot with H100, B200, Rubin, a Blackhole p150 and a WSE-3 ridge point, with decode (≈1 FLOP/B) and prefill bands overlaid.

## Chapter 3 — Making a chip out of a defective wafer

Every wafer has defects, so a wafer-sized chip must expect hundreds of flaws. Cerebras prints an ordinary array of reticle-sized dies, stitches them together with on-wafer wires across the scribe lines so the fabric is uniform across die boundaries, and handles defects with **redundant cores plus redundant fabric links**: the fabric is reconfigured to route around bad cores using spares ([HC31 slides](https://old.hotchips.org/hc31/HC31_1.13_Cerebras.SeanLie.v02.pdf); [STH HC34](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-wafer-scale-engine-wse-2-and-cs-2-at-hot-chips-34/)). The argument rests on small cores. Total area is about 50× a GPU while each core is about 100× smaller (WSE-3 core ≈ 0.05 mm², roughly 1/100 of an H100 SM), so each defect kills far less silicon. Cerebras's model at 0.001 defects/mm² has the wafer seeing 46 defects and losing **just 2.2 mm²**, which it presents as a "164× reduction in dead silicon area" and "~100× more fault tolerant" than a GPU [Vendor modelling; the two multipliers use different assumptions] ([Cerebras yield blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/100x-defect-tolerance-how-cerebras-solved-the-yield-problem)). Per-core area is uncertain: one source cites about 38,000 µm² from a WSE-3 whitepaper and another about 0.05 mm² ([hyper-accel](https://hyper-accel.github.io/en/posts/cerebras-wse/)).

For the course, the key consequence is software-visible. **Repair happens in the fabric, so the SDK sees a clean logical rectangle** (750×994 on CS-2) rather than a wafer with holes [Derived]. The per-core area splits about **50:50 between logic and SRAM** ([HC34 slides](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf)), and wires between cores are under 1 mm, which keeps data-movement energy low ([STH HC34](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-wafer-scale-engine-wse-2-and-cs-2-at-hot-chips-34/)). An academic counterpoint holds that a known-good-die approach such as Tesla Dojo may yield higher than die stitching ([Theseus, arXiv:2407.02079](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.02079)). A widely repeated claim of about 70,000 disabled cores per wafer comes only from Medium and is unverified.

Packaging solves three more problems. Power is delivered vertically into the wafer face, cooling uses a closed internal water loop (the CS-3 "engine block"), and a custom connector lets the wafer expand thermally without cracking. These details come from secondary summaries of the HC31 primary ([gpusmith](https://gpusmith.com/articles/en/cerebras-wafer-scale-engine-explained)). The CS-4 pushes power delivery further: AC/DC conversion sits **about 0.5 mm from the wafer versus about 50 mm on GPUs**, removing the PCB from the final power path [Vendor] ([STH HC2026 power delivery](https://www.servethehome.com/cerebras-talks-going-rack-scale-with-their-wses-at-hot-chips-2026/cerebras-cs-4-hot-chips-2026-power-delivery-continued/)). Spare-core ratios, wire pitch, voltage/current and coolant flow are not public.

## Chapter 4 — A PE is a 48 KB computer with a router attached

Each PE pairs a compute engine (CE) with a router. **The memory is 48 KB of SRAM in 8 single-ported 6 KB banks, single-cycle, interleaved by 32-bit word, and shared by code and data.** This size is unchanged across WSE-1, WSE-2 and WSE-3. Beside it sits a small software-managed cache for hot data such as accumulators: **256 B on WSE-2, 512 B on WSE-3** [Vendor] ([HC34](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf); [HC2024](https://hc2024.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/72_HC2024.Cerebras.Sean.v03.final.pdf); bank layout from [arXiv:2605.07954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07954)). In practice the program cannot use all 48 KB. The SDK's `spmv-hypersparse` benchmark asserts per-PE use stays under 46 KB, and the bandwidth test uses a "46KB fifo", which implies about 2 KB goes to code and runtime in that configuration [SDK 2.10.1] ([EX/benchmarks/spmv-hypersparse/run.py](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/spmv-hypersparse/run.py)). A useful sanity check: 21 PB/s ÷ 900k ≈ 23 GB/s per core, consistent with a few 32-bit bank accesses per cycle [Derived].

The WSE-3 core has **16 general-purpose registers and 48 data structure registers (DSRs)** that hold DSDs. Its SIMD datapath is **8-way 16-bit for FP16/BF16 and 16-way 8-bit for INT8** [Vendor] ([HC2024](https://hc2024.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/72_HC2024.Cerebras.Sean.v03.final.pdf)), up from 4-wide FP16 on WSE-1/2 (secondary, [hyper-accel](https://hyper-accel.github.io/en/posts/cerebras-wse/)). WSE-2 GPR and DSR counts and the FP32 SIMD width were not found. The core runs **up to eight simultaneous tensor operations called microthreads** [Vendor] ([Cerebras architecture deep dive](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning)). The SDK pins down a generational difference here [SDK 2.10.1]. "On WSE-2, the queue ID of an input or output fabric DSD corresponds to the ID of the microthread in which that operation executes. On WSE-3, queue IDs and microthreads can be decoupled," and "Unlike WSE-2, the WSE-3 architecture exposes microthread IDs." Two operations may not use the same microthread at once ([EX/tutorials/topic-15-wse3-microthreads/README.rst](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-15-wse3-microthreads/README.rst)).

Sparsity is native because execution is dataflow-triggered. An arriving wavelet selects the handler instructions, and zeros are filtered at the sender, so no compute is spent on them ([HC34](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf); [Lie MICRO-54 keynote](https://microarch.org/micro54/media/lie-keynote.pdf)). This mechanism underlies the sparse-FLOPS headline numbers. The SDK shows the software side: since a wavelet payload is 32 bits, "we can use the lower 16 bits to contain data … [and] the upper 16 bits to contain the index of the value" [SDK 2.10.1] ([EX/tutorials/topic-04-sparse-tensors/README.rst](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-04-sparse-tensors/README.rst)). No public ISA encoding exists, and there is no separate instruction cache. Visual idea: an exploded PE showing 8 SRAM banks, the cache, GPR/DSR files, the SIMD lanes, 8 microthread slots and the 5-port router.

## Chapter 5 — Wavelets ride 24 colors one hop per cycle

The router has **five bidirectional ports: north, south, east, west, and the "ramp" to its own CE** ([HC34](https://hc34.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day2/Machine%20Learning/HC2022_Cerebras_Final_v02.pdf); [arXiv:2404.15888](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15888)). A link carries **one 32-bit word per clock, and a hop takes about one cycle**. The ramp takes about 2 cycles ([arXiv:2209.15040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15040); [arXiv:2605.07954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07954)). On WSE-3, a 32-bit message can cross between neighbours in a single cycle in each direction ([arXiv:2604.27985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27985)). Each wavelet carries a **5-bit color tag. 24 colors (IDs 0–23) are routable, and the other 8 are reserved**; the color selects both the route and the consuming task ([SDK docs](https://sdk.cerebras.ai/computing-with-cerebras), via search extract; [SpaDA, arXiv:2511.09447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09447)). The SDK library confirms `NUM_COLORS = 24` for both WSE-2 and WSE-3, and the color configuration registers are memory-mapped at `0x7f40–0x7f57` [SDK 2.10.1] (LIB/target/common.csl). Each color has dedicated input buffers at each router, so the fabric is lossless and deadlock-free, and congestion on one color does not block another ([arXiv:2209.15040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15040)). Two same-colored wavelets arriving in the same cycle is undefined behaviour ([arXiv:2605.07954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07954)), which is why kernels alternate colors in a checkerboard. The vendor aggregate fabric numbers (220 Pb/s WSE-2, 214 Pb/s WSE-3) match 32 bits × 4 directions × 2 × ~850k PEs × ~1 GHz ≈ 2×10¹⁷ b/s [Derived].

Routes are static. Per PE and per color, `@set_color_config` declares an rx set and a tx set over `{NORTH, SOUTH, EAST, WEST, RAMP}`, and a tx set may multicast, as in `.tx = .{RAMP, SOUTH}`, which delivers locally and forwards down a column [SDK 2.10.1] ([EX/tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst); [gemv-08 layout.csl](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-08-routes-3/layout.csl)):

```csl
const send_color: color = @get_color(0);
@set_color_config(0, 0, send_color, .{.routes = .{ .rx = .{RAMP}, .tx = .{EAST} }});
@set_color_config(1, 0, send_color, .{.routes = .{ .rx = .{WEST}, .tx = .{RAMP} }});
```

Three runtime mechanisms add flexibility, all [SDK 2.10.1]. **Switches** give each color up to four route positions. A control wavelet advances the position, but only after it has passed, so its own payload goes to the old route ([topic-06](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-06-switches/README.rst), [topic-07](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-07-switches-entrypt/README.rst)). The library sets `SWITCH_REGS_PER_COLOR` to 1 on WSE-2 and 4 on WSE-3. **Filters** let a PE accept only wavelets whose upper 16 bits fall within a range, so each recipient takes its slice of a broadcast stream. WSE-2 has two filter banks and WSE-3 has one ([topic-08](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-08-filters/README.rst); LIB/target). **Color swap** (`swap_color_x`, flipping the lowest color bit on west-to-east hops) works only on WSE-2: "CSL currently does not support color swap on WSE-3" ([topic-14](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-14-color-swap/README.rst)).

Colors are the scarcest resource in a CSL program. memcpy takes 21–23, plus 20 for context switching, so the 25-point stencil benchmark notes that it "uses 21 colors … and SdkRuntime reserves 6 colors, so only 4 colors are left" [SDK 2.10.1] ([EX/benchmarks/25-pt-stencil/README.rst](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/25-pt-stencil/README.rst)). Router buffer depths are not public. Visual idea: an animated 4×4 mesh with a red/blue checkerboard reduction chain and a column multicast.

## Chapter 6 — There is no instruction stream, only tasks waiting for data

A PE has no OS and no program counter marching through `main`. Code is a set of **tasks bound to task IDs, and the hardware "picks the next task to run from those that have been activated and unblocked"** ([SDK docs](https://sdk.cerebras.ai/computing-with-cerebras), via search extract). There are three kinds, all [SDK 2.10.1]. **Data tasks** (wavelet-triggered tasks) run once per arriving wavelet and receive its payload as their argument. **Local tasks** are activated by `@activate` or by the completion of an async DSD operation. **Control tasks** are activated by a control wavelet carrying their ID, with 16 bits of optional payload ([topic-03](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-03-streaming-wavelet-data/README.rst); [topic-07](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-07-switches-entrypt/README.rst)). Tasks run to completion and cannot call each other, only activate one another. `@block` and `@unblock` provide back-pressure, and a non-routable control task ID can act as a "sentinel" marking the end of a stream ([topic-05](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-05-sentinels/README.rst)). A PE can even trigger its own data task by sending wavelets up the ramp and straight back down (the loopback trick in [topic-09](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-09-fifos/README.rst)).

The WSE-3 changes to the task model are the main portability hazard for the course, and each row below is [SDK 2.10.1] ([topic-05](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-05-sentinels/README.rst), [topic-15](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-15-wse3-microthreads/README.rst), [topic-16](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-16-queue-flush/README.rst), LIB/target):

| Mechanism | WSE-2 | WSE-3 |
|---|---|---|
| Data task ID source | Color, IDs 0–23 | Input queue bound to a color, IDs 0–7 |
| Local task IDs | 0–30 | 8–30 |
| Input / output queues | 8 / 6 | 8 / 8 |
| Output queue binding | Fabout DSD names the color; a queue can carry several colors | Each queue bound to one color via `@initialize_queue`; reuse needs `@queue_flush` and teardown task 29 |
| Microthread ID | Equal to queue ID | Explicit `.ut_id` (0–7), decoupled from queue |
| Fabric inputs per DSD op | Multiple allowed | At most one `fabin_dsd` |
| Color swap | Supported | Not supported |
| Switch registers per color | 1 | 4 |

Two small inconsistencies should be noted rather than smoothed over. The topic-05 README says WSE-2 data task IDs run 0–23, while a task-map comment in the topic sources says "0 through 24". A secondary blog's "up to 28 task IDs per PE" ([wafer.substack](https://wafer.substack.com/p/breaking-down-the-cerebras-wafer)) does not match the SDK's 0–30 local range. The SDK should win. Scheduler priority and arbitration policy are not documented locally (`<tile_config/task_priority>` exists in LIB but no tutorial explains it). Visual idea: fabric → router color lookup → ramp → input queue (WSE-3) or color (WSE-2) → task table → "activated ∧ unblocked" picker → microthread.

## Chapter 7 — DSDs turn a loop into one instruction

Without caches or DRAM, the hardware needs another way to make memory access cheap: **data structure descriptors, hardware address generators held in DSRs**. A memory DSD describes an affine walk over local SRAM, given either as base address plus extent and stride or as a `tensor_access` expression with an induction variable, a bound and an affine index. A fabric DSD (`fabin_dsd`/`fabout_dsd`) describes a stream of wavelets on a queue or color. A single builtin such as `@fmacs` (f32 FMA of vector by scalar), `@fadds`, `@fmovs`, `@fnegs`, `@mov16/32` or `@add16` then iterates over the whole descriptor [SDK 2.10.1] ([EX/tutorials/gemv-02-memory-dsds/README.rst](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-02-memory-dsds/README.rst)). A Cerebras patent describes DSDs the same way, as holding an operand's location, element count and addressing ([US 11580394](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11580394)). The canonical GEMV inner loop:

```csl
var A_dsd = @get_dsd(mem1d_dsd, .{ .tensor_access = |i|{M} -> A[i*N] }); // column, stride N
for (@range(u16, N)) |i| {
  @fmacs(y_dsd, y_dsd, A_dsd, x[i]);            // y += A[:,i] * x[i]
  A_dsd = @increment_dsd_offset(A_dsd, 1, f32); // next column
}
@fadds(y_dsd, y_dsd, b_dsd);                     // y += b
```

Fabric DSDs make communication and compute the same operation. `@fadds(y_dsd, y_dsd, in_dsd, .{ .async = true, .activate = exit_task_id })` accumulates values straight off the fabric into memory. In gemv-08 a middle column runs `@fadds(out_dsd, y_dsd, in_dsd)`: it receives a partial sum from the west, adds its local vector and forwards the result east in one instruction, a systolic reduction [SDK 2.10.1] ([gemv-06](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-06-routes-1/README.rst); [gemv-08 pe_program.csl](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-08-routes-3/pe_program.csl)). With `.async = true`, the operation runs in a microthread, and `.activate`/`.unblock` name the task to fire on completion, so callbacks are task IDs. This matters in practice. pipeline-01 notes the kernel "stalls if a blocking instruction `@add16(outDsd, inDsd, 1)` is used", because the CE must keep resources free to run memcpy, and a fabric-input DSD needs its color's task blocked, or the empty task will consume wavelets first ([pipeline-01 README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/pipeline-01-basic/README.rst)).

**FIFOs** extend the "small hardware queues used for fabric communication" with an SRAM buffer (`@allocate_fifo`) that one async op fills and another drains. A FIFO "requires two microthreads and a scratch buffer" ([topic-09](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-09-fifos/README.rst)). FIFOs are also the portability fix for WSE-3's one-fabric-input rule: "WSE3 does not allow multiple fabric inputs per DSD operation. Therefore, we introduce a FIFO for portability" ([sdklayout-03 add2vec.csl](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/sdklayout-03-ports-and-connections/add2vec.csl)). Hardware queues really are small: pipeline-01's single-PE redirect "stalls when size exceeds 14". **`@map`** applies a user function element-wise over DSDs and scalars (for example `@map(math_lib.sqrt_f32, dsdA, dsd_out)`), which "can lead to significant performance gains compared to writing explicit loops" ([topic-10](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-10-map-builtin/README.rst)). `mem4d_dsd`, the full DSD-op table and the DSD bit formats are documented only online and were not verified. One teaching device comes from the SDK itself: the single-tile matvec benchmark reports both "relative" bandwidth (what a cached architecture would need) and "absolute" bandwidth (actual SRAM accesses on the WSE), which shows students concretely that no cache absorbs reuse ([single-tile-matvec README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/single-tile-matvec/README.rst)).

## Chapter 8 — Getting data on the wafer runs at about 7 Gb/s per channel

The host link is the narrowest pipe in the system. The SDK benchmark says it plainly: "The host connects the device via 100Gbps ethernets. The data is distributed from/to couple of I/O channels. The maximum bandwidth of a single channel is around 7Gbps … the overhead of TCP is about 200 us." **At most 16 channels are available**, back-pressure from busy cores stalls I/O, and the runtime aggregates non-blocking commands to amortise TCP overhead [SDK 2.10.1] ([EX/benchmarks/bandwidth-test/README.rst](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/bandwidth-test/README.rst)). Sixteen channels at about 7 Gb/s is roughly 112 Gb/s, or 14 GB/s, so filling 44 GB of SRAM through one SDK host path would take more than three seconds [Derived]. That is why weights must either stay resident or be streamed by dedicated hardware (Chapter 9). CS-3 system Ethernet is reported as 12×100 GbE, but only on a forum citing Cerebras ([SemiWiki](https://semiwiki.com/forum/threads/the-new-cerebras-cs-3.19821/)). WSE-3T raises off-wafer I/O to 2.4 Tb/s per wafer [Vendor].

On the wafer, the `memcpy` library builds the I/O plumbing around the user's PEs. With `--memcpy`, `cslc` adds **west columns (command fan-out and host-to-device demux) and east columns (device-to-host mux)** around the program rectangle. That margin is why the user rectangle must be 7 columns narrower and 2 rows shorter than the fabric, and why tutorials compile with `--fabric-offsets=4,1`. A local compile of gemv-01 for WSE-3 produced `bin/out_0_0.elf` for the user PE, `west/bin/out_{0,1,2}_0.elf`, `east/bin/out_{0,1}_0.elf`, generated routing ELFs and `out_rpc.json` [SDK 2.10.1, local experiment] (LIB/runtime: `demux.csl`, `mux.csl`, `cmd_fan.csl`). memcpy claims a fixed set of resources, listed below [SDK 2.10.1] (LIB/memcpy/sys_params.csl):

| Resource | Reserved by memcpy |
|---|---|
| Routable colors | 21 `MEMCPYD2H_DATA`, 22 `RXCOMMAND`, 23 `MEMCPYH2D_DATA`; 20 `SYS_RXCTX` (context switch) |
| Local tasks | 24, 27, 28, 30; 29 = teardown; 31 = NOP |
| Control tasks | 33–37 (push, H2D, D2H, RPC, check-and-ignore), 40 (context config) |
| Queues | Input/output queue 0 (WSE-3 source comments also reserve queue 1; tutorials start user queues at 2) |

Data moves in two modes. **Copy mode** writes into an exported array on a rectangle of PEs. **Streaming mode** injects wavelets on the `MEMCPYH2D_*` colors along the top row and left column, where they trigger data tasks [SDK 2.10.1] ([gemv-09 README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-09-streaming/README.rst)). Commands are serialised on the RXCOMMAND color, so order matters. In gemv-09, "If the b values were streamed in before x, the program would hang." Users must not route memcpy colors themselves. `memcpy_h2d_rowbcast/colbcast` broadcast one column or row of host data to every PE in it, avoiding a 3× host-bandwidth waste, but work only in the host-to-device direction ([row-col-broadcast README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/row-col-broadcast/README.rst)). The physical count and location of wafer-edge SerDes are not public.

## Chapter 9 — Training streams weights in, inference pins them down

The system story has two inverse modes. Early CS-1/CS-2 work used **layer-pipelined** execution, with the whole model resident in SRAM and layers mapped spatially. From 2021, training moved to **weight streaming**: activations stay on the wafer, weights live in external **MemoryX** (DRAM plus flash, which also computes the weight update), and the whole wafer computes one layer at a time ([Cerebras weight-streaming blog](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/linear-scaling-made-possible-with-weight-streaming)). **SwarmX** "handles the broadcast of weights from MemoryX to the entire cluster and effectively reduces (sums) gradients in the opposite direction" ([Cerebras docs](https://training-api.cerebras.ai/en/2.0.3/wsc/cerebras-basics/how-cerebras-works.html)). Because the model size no longer depends on wafer SRAM, data-parallel scaling becomes, in Cerebras's framing, linear. Primary detail on the layer-pipelined mechanics was not found.

## Chapter 10 — CSL is Zig syntax wrapped around a hardware-resource type system

"CSL's syntax is like that of Zig. Despite the similarity, both the purpose and the implementation of the CSL compiler are different" [SDK 2.10.1] ([gemv-00 README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-00-basic-syntax/README.rst)). The scalar language is small. Types are `bool`, `i16/i32`, `u16/u32` and `f16/f32`, plus arrays (`[M*N]f32`, including multi-dimensional) and pointers. Every binding is `const` or `var`, and loops come in two forms, `for (@range(i16, n)) |i|` and `while (c) : (i += 1)`. Builtins start with `@` (for example `@as`, `@zeros` and `@constants`), and there are `comptime` blocks, enums, `switch`, `inline fn` and `anytype`. Two rules surprise newcomers: runtime functions take pointers rather than arrays (only comptime functions may pass arrays), and "CSL does not have a null pointer" ([topic-01 README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-01-arrays-and-pointers/README.rst)). The FP16 format is chosen at compile time with `--fp16-format=f16|cb16|bf16` (default f16; cb16 is "Cerebras float16"). The real content of the language is its hardware types: `color`, `input_queue`/`output_queue`, `data_task_id`/`local_task_id`/`control_task_id`, and `mem1d_dsd`/`fabin_dsd`/`fabout_dsd`. Most bugs come from misusing these, not the arithmetic [Derived from SDK].

**`param`** declarations are compile-time constants set by the layout or by `cslc --params=M:4,N:6`, so they can change "the program's compilation", not only its behaviour ([gemv-04 README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-04-params/README.rst)). Because every distinct parameter set produces another PE binary, gemv-07 replaces a per-PE `pe_id` param with runtime coordinates from `<layout>` (`get_x_coord()`), which "can reduce compilation time" ([gemv-07 README](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-07-routes-2/README.rst)). Modules are imported with `@import_module("<lib/name>", .{...})`, and each distinct parameter struct creates a separate instance. `@is_arch("wse3")` selects per-generation code inside `if` expressions, which is how one source file targets both WSE-2 and WSE-3.

A complete program has three parts [SDK 2.10.1] ([gemv-01](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-01-complete-program/README.rst)). The first is a **layout file**, whose `layout {}` block calls `@set_rectangle(w, h)` and `@set_tile_code(x, y, file, params)`, routes colors with `@set_color_config`, and declares host-visible names with `@export_name`. The second is one or more **PE programs**, which import `<memcpy/memcpy>` with the per-column parameters from `<memcpy/get_params>` and export symbols and functions with `@export_symbol`. The third is a Python **host script**. Grid coordinates are always (x = column, y = row), width by height.

```csl
// layout.csl
const memcpy = @import_module("<memcpy/get_params>", .{ .width = 1, .height = 1 });
layout {
  @set_rectangle(1, 1);
  @set_tile_code(0, 0, "pe_program.csl", .{ .memcpy_params = memcpy.get_params(0) });
  @export_name("y", [*]f32, false);            // host may read, not write
  @export_name("init_and_compute", fn()void);  // host-callable RPC
}
// pe_program.csl
param memcpy_params;
const sys_mod = @import_module("<memcpy/memcpy>", memcpy_params);
fn init_and_compute() void { initialize(); gemv(); sys_mod.unblock_cmd_stream(); }
comptime { @export_symbol(y_ptr, "y"); @export_symbol(init_and_compute); }
```

The rule students must learn first: **every host-callable function is an RPC that must end, directly or through a completion task, with `sys_mod.unblock_cmd_stream()`**. "The user must call unblock_cmd_stream() at the end of a RPC" (LIB/memcpy/memcpy.csl). If a single PE forgets, the next host command hangs. RPCs can take arguments (`@export_name("f", fn(u32)void)` with `runner.launch("f", np.uint32(n), nonblock=False)`, as in [25-pt-stencil](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/25-pt-stencil/README.rst)). The full language reference, including `comptime_float`, struct/union semantics and `linksection`, is online only and was not verified.

## Chapter 11 — The host drives the wafer through six calls

The host side is a Python library that wraps the device for both hardware and the simulator: `from cerebras.sdk.runtime.sdkruntimepybind import SdkRuntime, MemcpyDataType, MemcpyOrder` [SDK 2.10.1] ([gemv-03 run.py](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-03-memcpy/run.py)). The lifecycle is construct (`SdkRuntime("out", cmaddr=None)`, where `cmaddr=None` selects the simulator), `get_id(symbol)`, `load()`, `run()`, any sequence of `memcpy_h2d` / `launch` / `memcpy_d2h`, then `stop()`.

```python
runner = SdkRuntime(args.name, cmaddr=args.cmaddr)
A_symbol = runner.get_id('A'); runner.load(); runner.run()
runner.memcpy_h2d(A_symbol, A, 0, 0, 1, 1, M*N, streaming=False,
    order=MemcpyOrder.ROW_MAJOR, data_type=MemcpyDataType.MEMCPY_32BIT, nonblock=False)
runner.launch('init_and_compute', nonblock=False)
runner.memcpy_d2h(y_result, y_symbol, 0, 0, 1, 1, M, streaming=False, ...)
runner.stop()
```

The four integers are the (x, y, width, height) of the PE rectangle, followed by the element count per PE. **Distributing data is the host's job.** gemv-08 reshapes A with `A.reshape(ky, M_per_PE, kx, N_per_PE).transpose(0,2,3,1).ravel()` so that each PE receives a contiguous block, and it reads compile-time params back from `out/out.json` ([gemv-08 run.py](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-08-routes-3/run.py)). In **streaming mode** the host passes a color ID instead of a symbol (`memcpy_h2d(MEMCPYH2D_DATA_1, x, ..., streaming=True)`), and there is no `launch` at all: "computation is started by the wavelet-triggered tasks" ([gemv-09](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-09-streaming/README.rst)). Introspecting the module in the container shows the full API surface [SDK 2.10.1, local experiment]. The extras beyond the core calls are `call`, `read_symbol(x, y, name, dtype)`, `dump_core`, the stride and broadcast copy variants, and `send`/`receive` for streams. Every memcpy returns a `Task` handle, so `nonblock=True` plus `task_wait` is the asynchronous pattern. `MemcpyDataType` is 32-bit or 16-bit, and `MemcpyOrder` is row- or column-major. The exact multi-PE semantics of `COL_MAJOR`, and the difference between `call` and `launch`, are not explained in the tutorials.

**SdkLayout** is the newer Python-side alternative to `layout.csl`. It offers code regions (`create_code_region(file, name, w, h)`), symbolic `Color`s with automatic physical allocation (the compiler writes `colors.json`), `paint` for routes, ports joined by `layout.connect(tx, rx)`, which "will automatically find and encode a path", and host streams through `create_input_stream`/`create_output_stream` with `runtime.send`/`receive`. These programs run with `memcpy_required=False` and do not import `<memcpy/memcpy>`. Streams attach only to single-PE ports, so a multi-PE GEMV needs explicit demux/mux adaptor regions ([sdklayout-01..05](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/sdklayout-01-introduction/README.rst)). It fits the end of the programming unit, as the higher-level layer once students understand colors by hand.

## Chapter 12 — Compile, simulate, trace: the 2.10.1 toolchain end to end

The workflow is two commands [SDK 2.10.1] ([gemv-01 commands_wse3.sh](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/gemv-01-complete-program/commands_wse3.sh)):

```bash
cslc --arch=wse3 ./layout.csl --fabric-dims=8,3 --fabric-offsets=4,1 -o out --memcpy --channels 1
cs_python run.py --name out
```

`cslc` is a wrapper around a containerised `cslc-driver`. The wrapper handles `-o`, `--params`, `--colors`, `--memcpy`, `--channels` (up to 16), `--import-path` and `--width-west-buf`/`--width-east-buf`. The driver handles `--arch=wse2|wse3` (default wse2; wse1 retired), `--fabric-dims`, `--fabric-offsets`, `--fp16-format`, `--out-routes` (per-PE routing dump), `--disable-auto-microthread-id`, `--dump-dsr-alloc-graph`, `--g0` and an experimental `--separate-compilation`. The compile model, assembled from these artefacts, runs in seven steps [Derived from SDK]. First, `cslc` evaluates the layout at compile time. Second, it compiles each unique PE program to an ELF. Third, it adds the memcpy halo kernels and routing ELFs and writes `out_rpc.json`, which maps exported names to IDs. Fourth, `load()` places all ELFs on the simulator or the CS system. Fifth, `run()` starts the fabric. Sixth, each `memcpy`/`launch` becomes a command on color 22 that memcpy's control tasks (33–36) decode. Seventh, the simulator stops after 50 idle cycles.

The simulator is cycle-level and writes `sim.log`, `sim_stats.json`, `simconfig.json` and CTF traces in `simfab_traces/` into the working directory. One local run's stats read `"cycle_count": 1374, "fabric_x": 8, "fabric_y": 3, ... "sim_stop_cause": "Stopped due to idleness"`, and `sim.log` names the architecture with internal codenames, **`FYN` for WSE-2 and `SDR` for WSE-3** [SDK 2.10.1, local experiment]. Measured cycle counts, which include memcpy overhead, make a ready-made lab exercise:

| Example (local simulator run) | WSE-2 cycles | WSE-3 cycles |
|---|---|---|
| gemv-01 (1 PE) | — | 1,374 |
| gemv-08 (4×3 PEs on 11×5 fabric) | 4,023 | 4,539 |
| topic-13 (simprint) | — | 2,966 |

gemv-01 simulated in about 2 s of wall time. One environment gotcha: `cs_python` bind-mounts `$TMPDIR` over `/tmp`, so running from a directory under `/tmp` fails with "destination … doesn't exist in container" (compiling there works).

Debugging uses four tools [SDK 2.10.1]. The **`<simprint>`** library prints to `sim.log` with a cycle and PE prefix (`@1475 PE(0,0): sender beginning main_fn`) and is "particularly useful for debugging stalling programs", whereas **`<debug>`** trace buffers (`trace_u32`, `trace_timestamp`, read on the host with `debug_util(...).read_trace(x, y, 'trace')`) require the program to complete. Note that `read_trace` coordinates start at the fabric's northwest corner, not the rectangle's ([topic-12](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-12-debug-library/README.rst); [topic-13](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/tutorials/topic-13-simprint/README.rst)). **`csdb`** provides context, image, memory, register, trace and wavelet inspection, and connects to a CM or a core file. In 2.10.1 the older `sdk_debug_shell instr-trace`/`wavelet-trace` print "please use csdb" because traces are now CTF, though `sdk_debug_shell visualize` still renders routing in a browser. **`cs_readelf`** inspects ELFs (`--syms`, `--routing`, `--switching`, `--tile X,Y`). `<time>` provides TSC timestamps for in-kernel timing. The rest of the standard library includes `<math>` (f16/f32 sqrt, exp, tanh, ...), `<random>`, `<collectives_2d>` (MPI-like broadcast/scatter/gather/reduce_fadds), `<control>`, `<tile_config>`, `<malloc>` and `<message_passing>`. csdb's interactive CTF workflow and the browser visualiser were verified only from help screens.

For lab sequencing, the SDK's own tutorials form the spine [SDK 2.10.1]. The core sequence is gemv-00→09: syntax, complete program, memory DSDs, memcpy, params, multi-PE, first routes, 2×2 broadcast/reduce, k×k checkerboard, streaming. The event-driven streaming unit is pipeline-01..03 plus topic-03/05/09. Debugging is topic-12/13. Advanced routing and collectives are topic-06/07/08/11. WSE-3 differences are topic-15/16 (with topic-14 as the WSE-2-only contrast), and sdklayout-01..05 covers the higher-level API. Every tutorial ships both `commands_wse2.sh` and `commands_wse3.sh` except topic-14 and topic-15. The benchmarks give capstone material: 25-pt and 7-pt stencils, CG/BiCGSTAB/PCG, Cholesky, FFT 1D/2D/3D, GEMM and GEMV with collectives, SpMV hypersparse, game of life (8 colors = 4 directions × 2 parities), histogram-torus, bandwidth-test and others ([benchmarks directory](file:///home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks)).

Tenstorrent sits between the wafer and the GPU. A **Tensix tile** holds 1464 KiB of software-managed L1 (no caches), 2 NoC interfaces and **five RV32IM "baby" RISC-V cores that do not compute but issue work**. BRISC and NCRISC move data, while TRISC0/1/2 drive unpack, math and pack. The Tensix coprocessor has 2 unpackers, a matrix FPU, a 32-lane FP32 SFPU vector unit, a scalar unit and 4 packers ([tt-isa-documentation TensixTile](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tenstorrent/tt-isa-documentation/main/WormholeB0/TensixTile/README.md); [METALIUM_GUIDE.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tenstorrent/tt-metal/main/METALIUM_GUIDE.md)). All compute operates on **32×32 tiles** (a bf16 tile is 2048 B), tensors are padded to that size, and the DST accumulator holds 8 tiles in BF16 or 4 in FP32. Block floating point (bfloat8_b, bfloat4_b) and math-fidelity knobs (LoFi/HiFi2/HiFi4) trade accuracy for throughput ([tt-metal LLMs report](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tenstorrent/tt-metal/main/tech_reports/LLMs/llms.md)).

Each chip has **two separate 2D-torus NoCs running in opposite directions** (by convention NoC0 reads and NoC1 writes). A flit is 32 B, one flit per cycle per link, packets carry up to 8 KiB, latency is about 9 cycles router to router, and rectangular multicast is supported, extended on Blackhole with strided and L-shaped patterns ([WormholeB0 NoC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tenstorrent/tt-isa-documentation/main/WormholeB0/NoC/README.md)). That is about 32 GB/s per link per direction at 1 GHz [Derived]. Scale-out uses **on-die Ethernet with no switch fabric**: 16×100 GbE on Wormhole and 14×400 GbE on Blackhole, wired into meshes from n300 (2 chips) through QuietBox and T3K to the 32-chip Galaxy (an 8×4 mesh), and across hosts with TT-Fabric ([METALIUM_GUIDE.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tenstorrent/tt-metal/main/METALIUM_GUIDE.md)).

**TT-Metalium** is an open-source, OpenCL-like C++ host API with C++ device kernels. A typical operation runs three kernels per core, a reader on NoC0, a compute kernel compiled into three TRISC binaries, and a writer on NoC1, passing tiles through L1 **circular buffers** (up to 32 per Tensix, sized for at least 2 tiles to double-buffer). There is no `get_global_id()`; work is split by explicit runtime args, and even idle cores must receive them. The stack above it is TT-NN (PyTorch-like ops), TT-MLIR and TT-Forge (TT-XLA via PJRT for PyTorch/JAX), with ttsim as the ISA simulator ([METALIUM_GUIDE.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tenstorrent/tt-metal/main/METALIUM_GUIDE.md); [tt-forge](https://github.com/tenstorrent/tt-forge); [TT-Metalium eltwise docs](https://docs.tenstorrent.com/tt-metal/latest/tt-metalium/tt_metal/examples/eltwise_binary.html)). The guide explicitly rejects the GPU recipe of SIMT, latency hiding and caches in favour of asynchronous I/O and software pipelining, and compares the design to IBM CELL. Exact circular-buffer function names (`cb_reserve_back`, `cb_push_back` and so on) should be verified against guide code before quoting.

## Chapter 14 — An H100 has 132 SMs feeding on 3.35 TB/s of HBM

The NVIDIA baseline is a cache hierarchy over HBM. The **H100 SXM5 has 132 SMs (114 on PCIe), each with a 256 KB register file and 256 KB of combined L1/shared memory (up to 228 KB shared), a 50 MB L2 in two partitions, and 80 GB of HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s** [Vendor] ([Hopper whitepaper mirror](https://www.hpctech.co.jp/assets/images/info/catalog/pdf/gtc22-whitepaper-hopper_v1.02.pdf)). Chips and Cheese measured about 3.8 TB/s of aggregate L2 read bandwidth [Indep] ([Chips and Cheese](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/nvidias-h100-funny-l2-and-tons-of-bandwidth)). Dense BF16 is about 989 TFLOPS, and the often-quoted 1,979 TF is a 2:4-sparsity figure, the GPU analogue of Cerebras's sparse 125 PF. The SM-internal breakdown (4 sub-partitions, 128 FP32 lanes, 4 Tensor Cores, 2,048 threads per SM, NVLink 4 at 900 GB/s) is from the whitepaper but was not re-verified in this research.

|---|---|---|---|
| Memory | 80 GB HBM3, 3.35 TB/s | 192 GB HBM3e (~180 usable), ~8 TB/s | 288 GB HBM4, 19.2 TB/s (launch spec 22) |
| Low-precision peak | ~989 TF BF16 dense | ~9 PF FP4 dense (18 sparse) | 50 PF NVFP4 inference |

Sources: [SemiAnalysis InferenceX B200](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/chips/b200), [Spheron B200](https://www.spheron.network/blog/nvidia-b200-complete-guide/), [The Register CES 2026](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/05/ces_rubin_nvidia/), [Wccftech Rubin statement](https://wccftech.com/nvidia-official-statement-on-vera-rubin-spec-change/) and [pasqualepillitteri.it](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/27546/nvidia-site-confirms-vera-rubin-spec-cut-hbm4-nvlink-6). All are [Vendor] via secondary sources.

B200 joins two reticle-limited dies with a 10 TB/s NV-HBI link, which is NVIDIA's version of escaping the reticle limit that Cerebras escapes by stitching a whole wafer. The GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 GPUs and about 13.4 TB of HBM3e at 576 TB/s aggregate ([nextpcb](https://www.nextpcb.com/blog/nvidia-gb200-nvl72-architecture)). **The Rubin revision is a ready-made "vendor numbers are targets" case study.** NVIDIA's page now lists 19.2 TB/s HBM4 and 3 TB/s NVLink 6, down from the launch figures of 22 and 3.6, and NVIDIA says these "reflect what the system sustains at quality, volume production."

CUDA is **SIMT and bulk-synchronous**. A kernel is a grid of thread blocks, each block lives on one SM and shares its shared memory, and threads execute in warps of 32 in lockstep. A fully divergent warp runs at 1/32 of peak ([Intro to CUDA](https://intro-to-cuda.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial/warps.html)). Performance depends on **coalescing** (thread i reads element i) and **occupancy** (enough resident warps that a stalled warp can be swapped for a ready one to hide HBM latency) ([NVIDIA occupancy webinar](https://developer.download.nvidia.com/CUDA/training/cuda_webinars_WarpsAndOccupancy.pdf)). The model brings data to compute through caches and hides latency with oversubscription, the opposite of the spatial "place compute next to data" approach of Chapters 4–7.

The capacity–bandwidth trade-off is the throughline. A GPU has large capacity (80–288 GB per device) and medium bandwidth. A Cerebras wafer has small capacity (44 GB) and enormous local bandwidth. Tenstorrent has small SRAM, cheap GDDR, and reaches for scale-out. Galaxy Blackhole's 6.2 GB of SRAM across 32 chips is about one-seventh of one WSE-3's 44 GB, but Galaxy adds 1 TB of GDDR6 [Derived]. A 70B FP16 model (≈140 GB) fits on one B200 or two H100s, but needs four CS-3 wafers ([Spheron Cerebras vs H100](https://www.spheron.network/blog/cerebras-vs-nvidia-h100-inference-2026/)).

### Programming model

| | CUDA | CSL (Cerebras SDK 2.10.1) | TT-Metalium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Execution | SIMT; grid → blocks → warps of 32; time-multiplexed | Spatial dataflow; one program per PE or region; tasks fire on wavelet arrival or activation | Per-core MIMD; reader / compute / writer kernels on 5 baby RISC-Vs |
| Data movement | Loads/stores through caches; programmer tiles into shared memory | Wavelets on statically routed colors; fabric DSDs; memcpy for host | Explicit NoC DMA between DRAM and L1 and core to core |

### Scaling

NVIDIA uses a proprietary scale-up fabric (NVLink/NVSwitch, a 72-GPU domain at ~130–216 TB/s) plus a separate InfiniBand or Ethernet scale-out network, and combines tensor, pipeline and data parallelism. Tenstorrent uses one commodity Ethernet fabric for both scale-up and scale-out, which is cheaper but has lower per-link bandwidth. Cerebras does its "scale-up" on the wafer itself, since the wafer behaves like a rack of chips joined by on-silicon wires. Between wafers it uses SwarmX/MemoryX for training (to 2,048 CS-3s [Vendor]) and Ethernet pipeline placement for inference, with switchless Direct Wafer Links arriving in the CS-4 [Derived synthesis; sources in Chapters 9, 13, 14].

## Conclusion

The most useful idea for a course is that "wafer-scale" is less about size than about removing the memory hierarchy. Once every byte sits one cycle from its compute, the programming model has to change: there are no loads, only descriptors; no threads, only tasks waiting on data; and no shared memory, only messages on 24 colors. That is why CSL feels alien to CUDA programmers, and why the WSE-2 → WSE-3 changes that matter to software (queue-bound data tasks, explicit microthreads, one fabric input per op, no color swap) are bigger than the hardware changes that matter to marketing (2× FP16 SIMD, 6% more cores). The same logic explains the two system modes: weight streaming for training and SRAM-pinned pipelines for inference are both workarounds for 44 GB of capacity, and the CS-4's three-wafer rack and the roadmap's "3D wafer-scale SRAM" show Cerebras attacking that capacity limit directly.

For course design, the evidence supports a precise claim rather than a sweeping one. Cerebras is independently fastest per user on LLM decode and on strong-scaling science that fits on a wafer. It is not shown to be cheaper per token at scale or faster at everything. Tenstorrent is a cost-and-openness bet whose hardware and software are still settling. GPUs keep winning on capacity and economics while their FLOP-to-byte gap widens. Course materials will hold up best if they teach the roofline first, label every number by generation and provenance, and treat any dense-FLOPS figure for a Cerebras wafer as an estimate, since Cerebras has never published one.
