Chapter 8 · Part II · The machine

Systems: CS-3, MemoryX, SwarmX, CS-4

A WSE-3 wafer has 44 GB of on-chip SRAM Vendor. Frontier models need hundreds of gigabytes to terabytes. Cerebras crosses that gap in two opposite ways. For training it streams the weights through the wafer, one layer at a time. For inference it pins the weights onto several wafers and passes each token through them. (Neither has anything to do with the SDK's "streaming mode" from Chapter 7.)

After this chapter you can

  • Walk through one weight-streaming training step, say what MemoryX and SwarmX store and compute, and show with numbers why streaming weights does not starve the wafer.
  • Explain how an inference wafer runs a model as a pipeline of layer regions, where the KV cache lives, and why per-user speed and total throughput are different numbers.
  • Estimate how many wafers a model needs, including its KV cache, and the bandwidth ceiling on its decode speed.
  • Place CS-1 to CS-4, and the CS-5/CS-6 roadmap, on a timeline with the right numbers.

The system around the wafer

A CS-n is the system (power, cooling, I/O) built around one WSE-n wafer. CS-4 breaks the pattern: it is a rack holding three WSE-3T wafers, the higher-clocked WSE-3 variant from Chapter 2. Three execution modes run on these systems. Each one is a different answer to the 44 GB limit.

Training: weight streaming

From 2021, Cerebras trains large models by weight streaming. The activations stay on the wafer. The weights live off the wafer in MemoryX, a DRAM-plus-flash service that also performs the weight update. The whole wafer computes one layer at a time. SwarmX "handles the broadcast of weights from MemoryX to the entire cluster and effectively reduces (sums) gradients in the opposite direction" Vendor. The training docs put it in one line: "The compiler maps one kernel (layer) at a time onto the entire WSE, first for the forward pass and then for the backward pass" Vendor.

Systems

One training step on a weight-streaming cluster Vendor (white paper and training docs):

  1. The batch is cut into equal shards, one per system. "This technique is known as data parallelism." The shard is layer 1's input.
  2. Forward pass. MemoryX streams layer 1's weights, and SwarmX broadcasts one copy to every wafer. Each wafer computes layer 1's activations and keeps them in SRAM. Then layer 2's weights arrive, and so on. MemoryX starts sending layer k+1 before layer k has finished, so the latency of the off-wafer trip is hidden.
  3. At the loss layer each wafer computes the loss delta. SwarmX also sums the loss values and sends them to the user node.
  4. Backward pass, last layer first. Each layer's weights are streamed again, now in transposed order (MemoryX keeps both views of one copy). The wafer uses its stored activations to compute activation gradients and weight gradients. The weight gradients stream out in FP32, SwarmX sums them across wafers, and MemoryX applies the optimizer update as they arrive. The last layer's weights "can be retained to save time".
  5. The critical path is layer 1: its weights get their gradients last and are needed first in the next step. The wafer emits each layer's gradients in the same order the weights were sent, so updated weights can start streaming before layer 1's gradients are complete. The round trip "can be completely hidden when the compute time of the first layer is greater than the round-trip latency".
  6. At intervals, all weights are pulled from MemoryX to the user node as a checkpoint.

Per step, each parameter crosses the link twice inward as FP16 (forward and backward) and once outward as an FP32 gradient: "32 bits of data are sent in each direction per parameter" Vendor. The activations of every layer must stay on the wafer until the backward pass uses them, so wafer SRAM still limits the batch per wafer. It no longer limits the size of the model.

Why streaming does not starve the wafer

Chapter 7 left a puzzle: off-wafer data is slow, so "data that matters must stay on the wafer, or arrive through dedicated hardware (MemoryX in training)". Here is the dedicated hardware. The white paper says "The WSE-2's 1.2Tb/s of I/O bandwidth is used for receiving weights from and transmitting gradients back to the MemoryX service" Vendor, and HC2024 gives the same 1.2 Tb/s for CS-3 Vendor. That is 150 GB/s, about 10× the SDK host path of Chapter 7, but still about 140,000× less than the wafer's 21 PB/s of SRAM bandwidth Derived. It works because of Chapter 1's arithmetic intensity: in training, every weight that arrives is used once for every token in the wafer's shard.

Worked example: tokens per weight
  • Link: 1.2 Tb/s = 150 GB/s. At 2 bytes per FP16 weight, that is 75 billion weights per second into the wafer Derived.
  • Each arriving weight does one multiply-add (2 FLOP) per token in the shard. With T tokens per shard, that is 2T FLOP per weight, or about T FLOP per byte.
  • To keep a WSE-2 busy at its 7.5 PF FP16 dense peak (Hot Chips 2022 Vendor): 7.5×10¹⁵ ÷ (2 × 75×10⁹) ≈ 50,000 tokens per shard Derived. Below that the link is the bottleneck; above it, compute is.
  • The white paper says "batch shards typically contain hundreds of thousands of tokens" Vendor, comfortably past that knee.
  • Decode has T = 1 per sequence. The same link would then feed only 150 GFLOP/s, about 0.002% of the wafer Derived. That is why inference does the opposite.

Link-bound or compute-bound?

Weights arrive over the 1.2 Tb/s link; each one is reused for every token in the shard. The curve is utilisation = min(1, 2 × T × 75 G weights/s ÷ dense peak). It ignores the backward pass, which does twice the compute per weight and so is easier to feed. All outputs Derived.

A layer on the wafer

What does "the whole wafer computes one layer" mean at the level of the PEs from Chapters 3–6? Hot Chips 2022 and the white paper describe it Vendor. The activation tensor [B][S][H] is spread over the whole wafer, each core holding a tile: features H across the x axis, batch and sequence positions down y. Weights never get a home on the wafer: "No memory used for weights, not even stored temporarily." Instead each weight travels as a 32-bit fabric packet, "weight (16b) + index (16b)". It is multicast down the column of cores that holds the matching features, and its arrival triggers an FMAC against each core's local activations. That is one AXPY per weight, and exactly the data-triggered task of Chapter 5 and the DSD FMAC of Chapter 6. Then a PSUM command makes each row of cores ring-reduce its partial sums, and an FSUM command sends the final sum to the column that owns that output feature. Reductions overlap with the next weights. Zero weights are simply never sent, which is where Cerebras's "native unstructured sparsity" comes from.

Table Vendor. GPT-3 at 175B × 20 B = 3.5 TB, which is why the white paper sets a 4 TB minimum, and 120T × 20 B = 2.4 PB, the top of its range Derived. The CS-3 blog's 1,200 TB for "24 trillion parameters" implies 50 B per parameter, so the assumptions changed between eras Derived.

MemoryX uses "DRAM and flash storage in a hybrid fashion", and its storage and I/O "match or exceed the I/O bandwidth of a CS-2". It runs any optimizer, with a FLOP/s rate "three orders of magnitude greater than its I/O bandwidth" Vendor. Its compute never becomes the bottleneck, because the update costs O(P) work for P parameters, while the wafer's work grows as O(B·S·P) with batch and sequence. For the same reason one MemoryX can serve a cluster of any size Vendor.

SwarmX: a broadcast–reduce tree

SwarmX is built from broadcast–reduce nodes, each "containing a set of 100Gb/s network interfaces" and able to do one 1:4 or two 1:2 broadcast–reduce operations, with "enough compute to perform the floating-point gradient reductions at line-rate" Vendor. The nodes form a bidirectional tree. Node count grows linearly with the number of systems N, and latency grows as log N. Because gradients are summed on the way up, one copy of the gradient tensor crosses each link, the same as the weights going down. The white paper's summary: "From the perspective of the MemoryX service, the SwarmX fabric looks just like a single CS-2 and from the perspective of the CS-2, the SwarmX looks just like the MemoryX service" Vendor. So "the same mapping is used for every layer across all systems", and adding systems is pure data parallelism. No system ever holds part of the model, because only one layer has to fit at a time and any layer fits on any wafer. Cerebras chose this deliberately: mixing streaming with tensor or pipeline parallelism adds activation traffic, so it sets both to 1. It calls the result "linear scaling". The published evidence is Andromeda: 16 CS-2s gave 15.87× one CS-2's throughput on a GPT-3 XL genomics workload Vendor. That is one vendor workload at 16 systems, not a general proof.

Before weight streaming: layer-pipelined mode

CS-1 and the original CS-2 installation used layer-pipelined execution, for models "below 1 billion parameters, which can fit entirely in the on-chip memory" Vendor. All the layers are loaded onto the wafer at once. The compiler "maps each kernel/layer of the network onto a region (a rectangle)", connects the regions with on-wafer data paths, and sizes and places them "to optimize the throughput". Activations flow forward from region to region, and activation gradients flow back. Weights stay in their region and are updated on the wafer at the end of each backward step. After an initial fill latency, "all the layers of the network enter a steady state of active execution" Vendor. Keep this picture in mind: 2024 inference brings back the same spatial idea.

Inference: pin the weights, pass the tokens

Inference flips the trade. Decode is bandwidth-bound (Chapter 1), so the winning move is to keep every weight in SRAM and never fetch it: "we don't need to access external memory to load parameters because they're already there" Vendor. The cost is capacity. Besides the weights, decode keeps a KV cache (Chapter 1): the attention keys and values of every earlier token, one copy per sequence, growing with context length. On a GPU it lives in HBM. On Cerebras it must fit in whatever SRAM the weights leave free. Llama 3.1-70B needs about 140 GB at 16-bit Derived, so at launch in August 2024 its 80 layers were spread across four CS-3 systems, each holding a contiguous block of 20 layers, with only activations passing between wafers Vendor (Hot Chips 2024).

Inside the pipeline

Hot Chips 2024 describes the mapping directly Vendor:

  • The opposite of a GPU. "GPU: multiple chips to run a single layer. Cerebras: fraction of a chip to run a single layer."
  • Regions. "Model layers are mapped to wafer regions", each sized "based on memory and compute requirements", with "model weights and KV cache stored locally in the region memory close to the compute". This is layer-pipelined mode again, now for decode.
  • One token per stage. "Each wafer region processes 1 token": a matrix × vector at local batch 1. The next region is physically adjacent, and the "last region output [is] cycled back to generate next token". That loop is the autoregressive decode of Chapter 1.
  • Many users fill the pipe. "Single user is using only a fraction of the total memory bandwidth", so other users' tokens occupy the other stages at the same moment, and "every user gets full performance".
  • Prefill. A prompt is known up front, so several of one user's prompt tokens can be in the pipe at once.
  • Across wafers. "Keep almost all high communication on wafer… Transfer only activations between wafers", over CS-3's "low latency RDMA over Ethernet". Llama 3.1-8B (16 GB) fits on one wafer.

Pipeline decode: one user versus many

Users in flight

This is pipeline parallelism. For a single sequence, each new token visits the wafers in order, so at any moment only one region of one wafer is working on it. The rest are kept busy only when other sequences are in flight. Adding users raises total throughput without slowing anyone, until the pipe is full or the KV cache runs out of SRAM. At 2,100 tok/s, one token's full trip through all four wafers takes about 476 µs Derived. The even 20-layer split is shown on the Hot Chips 2024 slide Vendor; ~35 GB of weights per wafer follows from it Derived, though the embedding and output layers make the real split slightly uneven.

Worked example: the inter-wafer budget
  • Per token, the data crossing each wafer boundary is one activation vector: Llama-70B's hidden size 8,192 Vendor (Meta's model config) × 2 B ≈ 16 KB Derived.
  • HC2024: "CS-3 IO is <5us; 4 hops required; <1% impact to latency" Vendor. The four hops are three wafer-to-wafer hops plus the return trip.
  • At 450 tok/s (launch), one token takes 2.2 ms. Four hops at 5 µs add 20 µs, about 0.9%. At 2,100 tok/s (0.48 ms per token), the same 20 µs is about 4% Derived. That is one reason CS-4 cuts the hop to "as low as 2 microseconds" Vendor.
  • Bandwidth: HC2024 budgets 100 Gb/s of CS-3's 1.2 Tb/s, "<10% of available" Vendor. One user at 2,100 tok/s moves only about 34 MB/s per boundary Derived; the headroom is for many users and prefill.

KV-cache arithmetic: what limits users and context

For Llama-3-70B (80 layers, 8 KV heads of width 128 Vendor, Meta config), the KV cache per token is 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 (K and V) × 2 B = 327,680 B ≈ 0.33 MB Derived, the same figure as Chapter 1. Four wafers hold 176 GB; minus 140 GB of weights leaves ~36 GB. Even if all of it held KV and none held activations, that is about 110,000 tokens of context in total across all users: roughly 13 users at 8K context, or less than one user at 128K Derived. On the GPU side the same KV cache sits in HBM next to the weights. This is why Hot Chips 2024 lists "KV cache optimizations" among the next techniques Vendor, and why long-context deployments such as Llama 3.1-405B at 128K need many wafers. Worked example: where the decode speed limit actually is
  • At batch 1, every token reads all 140 GB of Llama-70B's weights. One H100 (3.35 TB/s) therefore tops out near 3.35 TB/s ÷ 140 GB ≈ 24 tok/s, and it cannot even hold the model. Eight H100s (26.8 TB/s) top out near 190 tok/s; the best measured DGX-H100 result in Hot Chips 2024 is 86 tok/s, at "<30% memory bandwidth utilization" Vendor, citing Artificial Analysis.
  • Four WSE-3s have 4 × 21 PB/s = 84 PB/s, a ceiling of about 600,000 tok/s. At 2,100 tok/s the wafers read weights at only ~0.3 PB/s, about 0.35% of the headline Derived. (Speculative decoding changes the bytes per accepted token, so this is approximate.)
  • Per byte held, a WSE-3 can read its whole 44 GB about 480,000 times per second; an H100 can read its 80 GB about 42 times Derived. That is "bandwidth per byte held", in numbers.
  • So on Cerebras, raw weight bandwidth is not the limit. Reductions, pipeline synchronisation, attention and KV reads, and the hops set the pace. The sources do not break this down.
Experiment: a real GEMV kernel's bandwidth, measured with the SDK

The headline 21 PB/s is a peak. The SDK benchmark benchmarks/single-tile-matvec computes y = A·x in fp32 with one N × N tile per PE, and reports bandwidth assuming an 850 MHz clock SDK 2.10.1. In a simulator run on a 2 × 2 rectangle (made for this course's review), N = 100 took about 10,160 cycles on both --arch wse2 and wse3 Measured. That is ≈ 4.0 B of useful weight per cycle per PE. The benchmark's own extrapolation to 750 × 994 PEs is ~2.5 PB/s of useful weight bytes (7.5 PB/s of raw SRAM accesses) Derived, roughly 12% of the headline. Even at that rate, 35 GB per wafer takes ~14 µs per token, about 56 µs for four wafers, a ceiling near 18,000 tok/s Derived, still well above the measured 2,100. As a cross-check on the headline, Hot Chips 2022 gives "2 full reads + 1 full write per cycle" of 64 bits per PE, i.e. 24 B/cycle; 850k PEs × 24 B × ~1 GHz ≈ 20 PB/s, the WSE-2 figure Derived.

Fast per user is not the same as cheap per token Pinning weights makes each user's decode fast. But a wafer serves relatively few concurrent sequences compared with a GPU running large batches Analyst, because each one's KV cache must fit in the SRAM the weights leave free. Analysts put batched H100 serving at ~$0.10–0.25 per million tokens Analyst, against Cerebras's launch list price of $0.60 for Llama 3.1-70B Vendor. Fast decode also does not shorten prefill. CS-4 and the roadmap

CS-4 keeps the WSE-3 silicon but rebuilds everything around it. The mechanism behind its "2× faster" claim is mostly power: conversion moved next to the wafer allows "twice as much power to the WSE-3 Turbo, enabling higher operating frequencies and faster token generation" Vendor.

The drop from <5 µs to 2 µs per hop matters more as tok/s rises (the inter-wafer budget above). One check with the widget: a CS-4 rack holds 3 × 44 = 132 GB, so even a whole rack cannot pin Llama-70B at 16-bit Derived.

CS-5, targeted for 2027, pairs Nexus with a next-generation WSE. Cerebras targets up to 10,000 output tok/s per user on gpt-oss-120b and Gemma 4 31B, up to 5,000 tok/s per user and 3 million tok/s per megawatt on multi-trillion-parameter models, and support for "models with more than 50 trillion parameters" Vendor. CS-6 integrates "wafer-scale SRAM and compute with 3D-stacked DRAM through ultra-high-bandwidth connections" Vendor. As ServeTheHome puts it, "Cerebras will be employing DRAM for the first time" Press. That is a direct attack on the capacity limit this whole chapter works around.

Where this sits for a CSL programmer Weight streaming and multi-wafer inference are run by Cerebras's machine-learning stack, not by programs you write with the SDK. That stack compiles a PyTorch graph into per-layer kernels and runs the same mapping on every system Vendor. The CSL SDK that the rest of this course teaches is a separate, lower-level path for custom kernels on one wafer (or the simulator), and its host path is the memcpy runtime of Chapter 7. The labs in Chapters 10–12 build the pieces such a mapping needs: data-triggered FMACs, broadcast along rows and columns, and reductions.

Check yourself

Why does weight streaming suit training but not low-latency inference?

In training, each weight arriving over the 1.2 Tb/s link is reused for every token in the shard; past about 50,000 tokens per shard a WSE-2 is compute-bound Derived. Decode uses each weight for only one token per sequence, so every token would wait on weights arriving over the off-wafer links (a few hundred GB/s per wafer, less than one GPU's HBM) Derived. Pinning weights in 21 PB/s SRAM avoids that.

How many times does each weight cross the MemoryX link in one training step, and in what format?

Twice inward as FP16 (forward pass, then backward pass in transposed order) and once outward as an FP32 gradient, which SwarmX sums across systems before MemoryX applies the update. That is 32 bits in each direction per parameter Vendor.

One user is generating with Llama 70B on four CS-3s. While wafer 3 processes their token, what are wafers 1, 2 and 4 doing?

Nothing for that user: the next token cannot start until this one leaves wafer 4 and loops back. The other wafers stay busy only with other users' tokens in flight. That is why per-user speed and total throughput are separate numbers.

Why can't the 36 GB left on a 4-wafer Llama-70B deployment serve 100 users at 8K context?

Each token of context costs ~0.33 MB of KV cache per sequence, so 100 × 8,192 tokens ≈ 268 GB, far more than 36 GB. About 13 users at 8K fit Derived.

Roughly how many WSE-3 wafers to hold a 405B model at 16-bit?

810 GB of weights ÷ 44 GB ≈ 19 wafers before any KV cache, or about 5 at 4-bit. In the widget, one 8K-context user (~4 GB of KV) still fits in 19; ten such users need 20 Derived.

A CS-4 rack has three 44 GB wafers. Can it hold Llama 70B at 16-bit with weights pinned?

No: 3 × 44 = 132 GB < 140 GB before any KV cache. At 8-bit (70 GB) it fits, with about 62 GB to spare Derived.
