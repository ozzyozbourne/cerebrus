Chapter 4 · Part II · The machine

The fabric: wavelets and colors

900,000 PEs Vendor, WSE-3 share no memory. The only way data moves between them is the fabric: a 2-D mesh of routers passing 32-bit messages called wavelets, about one hop per cycle on hardware (the WSE-3 simulator models 2; measured below), along routes fixed when the program is loaded. Programming the WSE is largely the art of choosing those routes.

After this chapter you can

  • Describe a wavelet, a color (a virtual channel), and a route (rx → tx) on one router, and explain why the fabric never drops data (back-pressure).
  • Estimate how long a stream takes to cross the wafer: distance costs latency once, length costs one cycle per word.
  • Read and write @set_color_config for point-to-point, pass-through, multicast and corner-turn routes, and know the WSE-3 rule of one input direction per color.
  • Explain what goes wrong when a reduction chain uses one color, and why programs alternate colors.
  • Use switches and filters to save colors, and plan a program's color budget.

A mesh of five-port routers

Every PE has a router with five bidirectional ports: NORTH, SOUTH, EAST, WEST to its neighbours, and RAMP down to its own compute engine (CE). A link carries one 32-bit word per cycle and a hop takes about one cycle; the ramp takes about two Indep (arXiv:2209.15040, 2605.07954). (Those are hardware-paper figures; the WSE-3 simulator charges 2 cycles per hop, measured below.) There are no long wires and no global bus. On a CS-2, whose SDK-visible fabric is 750 × 994 positions SDK 2.10.1, a wavelet from one corner to the other takes 749 + 993 = 1,742 hops, about 1.6 µs at the WSE-2's 1.1 GHz Vendor, HC22 or 2.0 µs at the 850 MHz the SDK benchmarks assume Derived.

A color is a virtual channel: a lane label carried by the wavelet, not a separate wire. All colors crossing one link share that link's one word per cycle, and the router interleaves them. What each color gets is its own route rule and its own small input buffer, so colors can be steered and stalled independently. (Think of one railway track carrying trains of different liveries, with the points at every junction set in advance for each livery.) More colors do not mean more bandwidth.

Nothing is dropped: back-pressure

What happens when a receiver is busy? Each router keeps a small buffer per color on each input. If the next hop's buffer for that color is full (say, the receiving CE is busy and not draining it), the wavelet waits where it is. The stall propagates upstream hop by hop until the sender's CE stops sending. The fabric is lossless: a slow receiver slows the sender, it never loses data Indep (arXiv:2209.15040). This is back-pressure. Because buffers are per color, a stalled color does not stop other colors from using the same link. Chapter 5 shows how tasks use this deliberately, and Chapter 6 shows that a fabric DSD op simply waits for its next wavelet. Try it below.

One link, two colors: sharing and back-pressure

Two senders both route EAST over the same link: c0 from the west, c1 from the north. The link carries one wavelet per cycle and alternates between colors that have data and room downstream. Tick "stall c0 receiver" and watch c0's buffers fill, its sender stop, and c1 keep flowing. Buffer depth 2 is illustrative. Router buffer depths are not published; the CE-side input queues are: memcpy.csl configures depths 6 / 4 / 2 for queues 0–1 / 2–3 / 4–7 SDK 2.10.1, and Lab 2 measures about 16 wavelets held per hop (Chapter 12).

A multicast waits for its slowest branch. A multicast route (a tx with several ports, below) copies each wavelet to every tx port. If one branch has no room, the wavelet does not go ahead down the others. I checked this on a 3-PE column with program 05's routes and M = 64. I kept the middle PE's CE busy for about 1,050 cycles before it began to receive. The bottom PE was ready from the start, yet it finished at 1,136 cycles instead of 96, and the root's send finished at 1,119 instead of 82. With M = 8, which the root pushed into buffers within 21 cycles, the bottom PE still waited until cycle 1,080. WSE-2 behaves the same way (bottom PE done at 2,154 behind a 2,074-cycle delay) Measured, SDK 2.10.1 simulator, each PE timed on its own TSC. So a broadcast such as Lab 2's (Chapter 12) runs at the pace of its slowest receiver.

Latency is paid once, length once per word

Wavelets pipeline. Sending a 1,000-element f32 vector 100 PEs east, the first wavelet arrives after about 2 (ramp) + 100 (hops) + 2 (ramp) ≈ 104 cycles, and the rest follow one per cycle behind it, so the whole vector lands after about 1,100 cycles: 1.0 µs at 1.1 GHz, 1.3 µs at 850 MHz Derived. Distance adds latency once; length costs one cycle per word. Each link carries 32 bits per cycle, which is 4.4 GB/s per direction at 1.1 GHz, or 3.4 GB/s at 850 MHz Derived. That is a fifth or less of the ~23 GB/s a PE gets from its own SRAM (Chapter 3). Moving a word to a neighbour is cheap, but keeping it local is cheaper.

I checked this on the SDK simulator. A row of P PEs sends M wavelets east through pass-through routers, and the last PE streams them straight back west on a second color. PE 0 times the round trip with its own timestamp counter, so no clocks have to be compared across PEs. Each extra PE in the row adds 2 hops (out and back). The slope came out at 2.0 cycles per hop on WSE-3 and 1.0 on WSE-2, and each extra wavelet adds 1 cycle on WSE-3 Measured, SDK 2.10.1 simulator.

A hop through a CE costs 4–6 router hops. A PE that receives, adds and forwards (Lab 2's chain) costs about 9.2 cycles per PE on WSE-3 and 6.0 on WSE-2, against 2.0 / 1.0 for a pure router hop Measured, round trip, Ch. 12 #hop-cost. (An older "~7 cycles per PE" came from comparing clocks on different PEs and is wrong.) Roughly, on WSE-3: one router hop (2) + down the ramp (≈ 2) + up the ramp (≈ 2) + the CE taking the wavelet from its input queue, adding, and handing it to its output queue (the remaining ≈ 3) ≈ 9 Derived; only the total is measured, the ramp figure is from the papers. That is the rule behind relaying, below: keep traffic in the routers (pass-through, multicast), and send it through a CE only when the CE has work to do on it.

Try it yourself: the hop-cost round trip

Three files in one directory outside /tmp (cs_python fails there). PE 0 sends M wavelets east on c0 through P − 2 pass-through routers; the last PE's CE relays them back west on c1; PE 0 stamps its TSC before the send and in the receive-completion task.

layout.csl
param P: i16; param M: i16;
const c0: color = @get_color(0);   // eastbound
const c1: color = @get_color(1);   // westbound
const memcpy = @import_module("<memcpy/get_params>", .{ .width = P, .height = 1 });
layout {
  @set_rectangle(P, 1);
  for (@range(i16, P)) |x| {
    const role: i16 = if (x == 0) 0 else if (x == P - 1) 2 else 1;
    @set_tile_code(x, 0, "pe_program.csl", .{ .memcpy_params = memcpy.get_params(x),
                   .M = M, .role = role, .c0 = c0, .c1 = c1 });
    if (role == 0) {
      @set_color_config(x, 0, c0, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{RAMP}, .tx = .{EAST} } });
      @set_color_config(x, 0, c1, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{EAST}, .tx = .{RAMP} } });
    } else if (role == 1) {   // pure router hops: the CE never sees these
      @set_color_config(x, 0, c0, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{WEST}, .tx = .{EAST} } });
      @set_color_config(x, 0, c1, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{EAST}, .tx = .{WEST} } });
    } else {
      @set_color_config(x, 0, c0, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{WEST}, .tx = .{RAMP} } });
      @set_color_config(x, 0, c1, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{RAMP}, .tx = .{WEST} } });
    }
  }
  @export_name("start", fn()void); @export_name("go", fn()void); @export_name("t", [*]u16, true);
}
pe_program.csl
param memcpy_params; param M: i16; param role: i16; param c0: color; param c1: color;
const sys_mod = @import_module("<memcpy/memcpy>", memcpy_params);
const tsc = @import_module("<time>");
var t = @zeros([2 * tsc.tsc_size_words]u16);   // start stamp, end stamp
var t_ptr: [*]u16 = &t;
var v: [M]f32; var w: [M]f32;
const v_dsd = @get_dsd(mem1d_dsd, .{ .tensor_access = |i|{M} -> v[i] });
const w_dsd = @get_dsd(mem1d_dsd, .{ .tensor_access = |i|{M} -> w[i] });
const oq: output_queue = @get_output_queue(if (role == 0) 2 else 3);
const iq: input_queue  = @get_input_queue(4);
const send_c = if (role == 0) c0 else c1;
const recv_c = if (role == 0) c1 else c0;
const out_dsd = @get_dsd(fabout_dsd, if (@is_arch("wse3")) .{ .extent = M, .output_queue = oq }
                                     else .{ .extent = M, .output_queue = oq, .fabric_color = send_c });
const in_dsd  = @get_dsd(fabin_dsd, .{ .extent = M, .input_queue = iq });
const done_id: local_task_id = @get_local_task_id(10);
const idle_id: local_task_id = @get_local_task_id(11);
task done() void { tsc.get_timestamp(@ptrcast(*[3]u16, &t[3])); sys_mod.unblock_cmd_stream(); }
task idle() void { }
fn start() void {
  tsc.enable_tsc();
  // the relay is armed before PE 0 sends, so launch skew is not timed
  if (role == 2) @fmovs(out_dsd, in_dsd, .{ .async = true, .activate = idle_id });
  sys_mod.unblock_cmd_stream();
}
fn go() void {
  if (role != 0) { sys_mod.unblock_cmd_stream(); return; }
  tsc.get_timestamp(@ptrcast(*[3]u16, &t[0]));
  @fmovs(w_dsd, in_dsd, .{ .async = true, .activate = done_id });   // arm the receive
  @fmovs(out_dsd, v_dsd, .{ .async = true });                        // then send
}
comptime {
  @bind_local_task(done, done_id); @bind_local_task(idle, idle_id);
  if (role != 1) {
    @initialize_queue(oq, if (@is_arch("wse3")) .{ .color = send_c } else .{});
    @initialize_queue(iq, .{ .color = recv_c });
  }
  @export_symbol(start); @export_symbol(go); @export_symbol(t_ptr, "t");
}

Routes are set at load time

For each PE and each color, the layout file declares a route: the input port a wavelet of that color is accepted from (rx) and the set of ports it is sent to (tx). It is configured when the program is loaded, and the router never looks anything up per wavelet. A PE can rewrite its own routes between phases (teardown, below), but there is no per-packet routing. Here is the route pair from Lab 2's row reduction (Chapter 12), sending from PE (0,0) to PE (1,0), written with @set_color_config:

layout.csl
const c0: color = @get_color(0);
// PE (0,0): accept from my own CE, send EAST
@set_color_config(0, 0, c0, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{RAMP}, .tx = .{EAST} } });
// PE (1,0): accept from WEST, deliver down the RAMP to my CE
@set_color_config(1, 0, c0, .{ .routes = .{ .rx = .{WEST}, .tx = .{RAMP} } });

The other half: how the CE gets onto a color. rx = RAMP only says "accept what my own CE sends on c0". The CE puts wavelets there, and takes them off, with a fabric DSD: a DSD that names a hardware queue (and so a color) instead of memory addresses (Chapter 6). Program 03 pairs every route with a few lines of pe_program.csl:

The router half decides where a color goes; the CE half binds a queue to that color (@initialize_queue; on WSE-2 the output DSD names the color itself) and moves the data. Neither half works alone. Chapter 6 explains the right-hand side, and Chapter 5 what runs when a wavelet arrives.

Why static? A packet-switched network puts a destination address in every message, and every router decodes it. Here the wavelet carries only a 5-bit color and the route is already in a register. That keeps all 32 bits for payload, keeps each hop to about a cycle, and keeps 900,000 routers tiny. The price is that every communication pattern must be planned at compile time, and every distinct pattern through a PE costs a color. Hold on to that: it is why colors become the scarcest resource at the end of this chapter. (Tenstorrent makes the opposite choice, with addressed packets, in Chapter 16.)

Four shapes cover most programs:

  • Point to point: rx RAMP → tx EAST at the sender, rx WEST → tx RAMP at the receiver.
  • Pass-through: rx WEST → tx EAST. The wavelet crosses the PE without touching its CE.
  • Multicast: rx NORTH → tx {RAMP, SOUTH}. One wavelet is copied to the local CE and forwarded, so one send reaches a whole column (Lab 2's broadcast). It moves at the pace of its slowest branch (above).
  • Corner turn: rx WEST → tx SOUTH. Routes need not go straight; a path can bend at any router.

Under the hood: one 16-bit register per color

Each color's route at a PE is one 16-bit configuration register SDK 2.10.1. Five tx bits (WEST, EAST, SOUTH, NORTH, RAMP) may be set in any combination; that is all multicast is. The rx field differs by chip. On WSE-2 it is five more one-hot bits, so a color may accept from several ports. On WSE-3 it is a 3-bit code naming exactly one port (or "disabled"). I compiled Lab 2's layout with .rx = .{WEST, NORTH}: --arch=wse2 accepted it, and --arch=wse3 stopped with error: expected at most 1 input direction(s) on 'wse3', got: 2 Measured, cslc 2.10.1. On WSE-3, multicast is free, but merging two inbound streams onto one color is impossible.

So how does many-to-one work? Three ways, all used later in the course. (a) Chain through the CEs: each PE receives on one color, adds, and sends on another, so the merge happens in arithmetic, not in the router (Lab 2's reduction, Chapter 12; about 9 cycles per PE on WSE-3, above). (b) One color per source: each input arrives on its own color and lands in its own queue (Lab 3's halo exchange uses 4, Chapter 13). (c) Change rx over time: switches or teardown re-point a color's input between phases, which is how <collectives_2d> reduces onto any root with two colors per axis (below; Chapter 14; the allreduce of Chapter 15).

Why programs alternate colors

A color's route at one router is one rx and one tx set, and the rule is "every wavelet arriving on an rx port goes to every tx port". Take a middle PE in a reduction chain. It must deliver the partial sum from the west to its CE, and send its own result east. On one color, the only way to say both is rx = {WEST, RAMP}, tx = {RAMP, EAST}. That route does the wrong thing twice. The partial sum from the west is copied east as well as down the ramp, so it skips this PE's addition. The PE's own result is copied back down its own ramp as well as east. The router has no notion of "this wavelet is mine, that one is passing through"; only the color separates traffic. (On WSE-3 the route will not even compile, because it has two rx ports. On WSE-2 it compiles Measured, cslc and then hangs or gives wrong sums: a one-color program 03 hung on the WSE-2 simulator Measured.) Run the simulator's Broken pattern to watch both failures.

The fix is to give the two roles different colors, alternating by column parity: even columns send on c0 and receive on c1, and odd columns do the reverse. Now each color has a simple one-in, one-out rule at every PE. The SDK and this course call this a checkerboard. Here it alternates by column only; a 2-D pattern alternates by row as well (figure below).

A separate hazard. If two wavelets of the same color reach one router in the same cycle from different rx ports, the behaviour is undefined Indep (arXiv:2605.07954). That is another reason never to merge streams on one color. The simulator flags it as a "collision".

Runtime flexibility: switches, filters and teardown

Control wavelets

A control wavelet is a wavelet with its ctrl flag set (a fabric output DSD with .control = true). Instead of plain data, its 32 bits carry commands for the routers it passes through, plus an optional control-task ID and 16 bits of data for the receiving CE SDK 2.10.1, <control>. A router that sees one executes its command: advance the switch, reset it, or enter teardown. If the command's CE-ignore bit is clear and the route reaches the RAMP, the CE also gets the wavelet, and the named control task runs with the 16-bit data (Chapter 5).

Switches: one color, several routes

A color can hold up to four route positions: the default (position 0) plus pos1–pos3. Both WSE-2 and WSE-3 support this, and the SDK's switches tutorial ships a build for each. The hardware just stores the state differently: SWITCH_REGS_PER_COLOR is 1 on WSE-2 and 4 on WSE-3. Not every color can switch: memcpy's source lists 0–9, 12, 13, 16, 17, 20 and 21 as switching-capable SDK 2.10.1. A control wavelet with SWITCH_ADV moves the router to the next position. The subtlety: "a PE router will move to a new switch position only after the control wavelet carrying the switch command passes through that PE", so the control wavelet itself follows the old route SDK 2.10.1, topic-07.

tutorials/topic-06-switches/layout.csl (centre PE (1,1))
@set_color_config(1, 1, channel, .{
  .routes   = .{ .rx = .{ RAMP }, .tx = .{ NORTH } },   // position 0
  .switches = .{ .pos1 = .{ .tx = WEST }, .pos2 = .{ .tx = EAST }, .pos3 = .{ .tx = SOUTH },
                 .current_switch_pos = 1, .ring_mode = true } });

Filters: one stream, many slices

A range filter lets a PE's CE accept only wavelets whose upper 16 bits fall in [min_idx, max_idx]. This is the same index field that Chapter 5 uses for sparse data. In tutorial topic-08, PE 0 sends 12 wavelets packed as (i << 16) | f16(10 + i). PEs 1–3 route rx WEST → tx {RAMP, EAST} (PE 3 only RAMP) with min_idx = 3k, max_idx = 3k + 2. So PE 1 keeps 3–5, PE 2 keeps 6–8 and PE 3 keeps 9–11, while the stream still flows on east, and indices 0–2 are kept by no one SDK 2.10.1, topic-08. The simulator's Range filter pattern replays it.

A counter filter passes one wavelet to the CE each time its counter wraps. memcpy uses one on every row to deal a host stream round-robin. For a row of width w it sets limit = w − 1 and starts PE p at (w − p) mod w, so on a row of 4 the stream x0 x4 x8 x12 x1 … is dealt PE 0: x0 x1 x2 x3, PE 1: x4 … x7, and so on SDK 2.10.1, memcpy/wse2/memcpyh2d.csl. Both chips have 6 filters: WSE-2 splits them into two banks (4 + 2), and WSE-3 has one bank of 6 with a reworked counter (pass and drop counts in half-wavelets) SDK 2.10.1.

Teardown: rewriting routes between phases

A color configured with .teardown = true can be put into teardown state by a TEARDOWN control wavelet. The router sets that color's bit in a teardown-pending register, which has one bit per routable color, and the CE runs local task 29. The handler rewrites the router (@set_local_color_config, plus tile_config calls for filters and switches) and then releases the color SDK 2.10.1, tile_config/teardown.csl, topic-16. This is how <collectives_2d> provides MPI-style broadcast, scatter, gather and reduce, rooted at any PE, with just two colors per axis SDK 2.10.1, topic-11. memcpy uses the same mechanism on its own colors. Never bind your own task to 29.

Reaching PEs you have no route to

The router never inspects destinations. If a destination is not on a configured path, a CE must receive the wavelet, decide, and send it again. The game-of-life benchmark gets diagonal neighbours this way: "each PE … also forwards the received state from its N and S neighbors to its E and W neighbors", so a PE hears its diagonals through the side links SDK 2.10.1, game-of-life. The histogram-torus benchmark folds a ring onto the mesh and "actively reroute[s]" each wavelet east–west after a north–south leg SDK 2.10.1. Each software hop costs ramp latency plus task time instead of one cycle, so these patterns are saved for irregular traffic.

Colors are the scarcest resource

Of the 24 routable colors, memcpy, the SDK's host-I/O runtime (Chapter 7), takes 20–23. That leaves 20 for your program SDK 2.10.1. Tick the patterns one program needs and watch the budget fill.

Color-budget planner

The real programs: Lab 1 = 1 color (its H2D stream on color 0), Lab 2 = 2 (c0/c1; the broadcast reuses color 0), Lab 3 = 4 course programs. memcpy reserves 21 (device→host data), 22 (command stream), 23 (host→device data) and 20 (used internally by the runtime for switching) SDK 2.10.1. The SDK's 25-point stencil benchmark spends 21 colors and task IDs on its communication, and its README says SdkRuntime "reserves 6 colors, so only 4 colors are left" for streaming transfers. (The library source names 4 routable system colors; the README's count of 6 is not broken down.) Every new pattern spends colors, which is why switches, filters, teardown and the SdkLayout API exist. SdkLayout is a Python layout layer that allocates colors for you (Chapter 10).

The vendor's 220 Pb/s for WSE-2 sits in that range Derived. The "× 2" counts each link once from each end, as vendors appear to. Each individual link, though, carries only 3.4–4.4 GB/s per direction Derived: the fabric is fast in aggregate, not between any two distant PEs.

A wavelet must go east along row 0 and then turn south down column 5. What is the route at PE (5,0)?

.rx = .{WEST}, .tx = .{SOUTH}, or .tx = .{RAMP, SOUTH} if (5,0) also needs the data.

How long does a 1,000-wavelet stream take to cross 100 PEs, and what dominates?

About 2 + 100 + 2 + 999 ≈ 1,100 cycles at 1 cycle per hop (≈ 1,200 at the WSE-3 simulator's 2). Length dominates: distance is paid once, because wavelets pipeline.

Why can't a middle PE in a reduction chain send and receive on the same color?

Because a color's rule is "any rx port → all tx ports". With rx = {WEST, RAMP} and tx = {RAMP, EAST}, the incoming partial sum would also be forwarded east un-added, and the PE's own output would loop back to its own CE. On WSE-3 the route does not even compile (two rx ports).

Why does .rx = .{WEST, NORTH} compile for wse2 but not for wse3?

WSE-2's route register has five one-hot rx bits. WSE-3's has a 3-bit code that names a single port, so cslc rejects a second direction.

A 2-D stencil exchanges with all four neighbours. Why does it need 8 colors, and how many are left?

4 directions × 2 parities = 8. 24 − 4 (memcpy) − 8 = 12 left for everything else.

In topic-06, why does the switch start at pos1 when the first data wavelet should go east (pos2)?

Each control wavelet travels on the current position and the switch advances only after it passes. The first control wavelet goes west (pos1, ignored by the CE) and flips the switch to pos2, so data 0 then goes east.
