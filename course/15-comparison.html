Head to head

Three answers to the memory wall. NVIDIA keeps a big off-chip memory and hides its latency. Tenstorrent puts large SRAM in each core, cheap DRAM beside it, and Ethernet everywhere. Cerebras removes off-chip memory from the wafer and puts every byte one cycle from its core. This chapter compares them on the axes that matter and states plainly what the evidence does and does not show.

Scaling

NVIDIA: NVLink/NVSwitch scale-up (72 GPUs per rack) plus InfiniBand/Ethernet scale-out; tensor, pipeline and data parallelism. Tenstorrent: one commodity Ethernet fabric on every die for both scale-up and scale-out, wired into switchless meshes — cheaper, lower bandwidth per link. Cerebras: "scale-up" happens on the wafer itself (it behaves like a rack of chips joined by on-silicon wires); between wafers it uses MemoryX/SwarmX for training (to 2,048 CS-3s) and pipelined placement over Ethernet for inference, with Direct Wafer Links arriving in CS-4 Vendor.

The fair reading Cerebras's independently confirmed advantage is per-user tokens per second and strong scaling on problems that fit on the wafer. The MD authors themselves cautioned that the result "doesn't mean the chip is faster overall than exascale systems like Frontier". GPUs win on throughput per dollar at high batch and capacity per device. Tenstorrent's case is cost and openness; no independent benchmark shows it beating GPUs on a headline metric. There is no independent three-way cost or energy-per-token study.

What programming the wafer taught us

Writing and validating five programs for this course made the trade-offs concrete:

  • The hard part is resources, not arithmetic. Every bug was a mutability flag, a command-stream race, or a microthread clash. None was a wrong formula.
  • Communication is cheap but not free. The stencil's ~88-cycle fixed cost per step (four one-word transfers plus task overhead) outweighed the compute until each PE held dozens of cells.
  • Weak scaling is effortless; capacity is not. Going from 8 to 16 PEs cost nothing per step. But every PE holds at most ~46 KB, and data enters through a ~7 Gb/s-per-channel door.
  • The simulator is a real asset. Repeatable, cycle-counted runs (totals varied by at most 2 cycles between reruns) let us find a race by recording state and pin per-step cost to within a cycle. That is harder to get on a GPU.

Choosing

Final check

Explain in two sentences why Cerebras is fast at single-user decode.

Decode reads every weight per token and does ~1 FLOP per byte, so it is limited by memory bandwidth. With weights pinned in on-wafer SRAM spread across ~900k PEs, the wafer reads them far faster than any HBM.

Why might a provider still serve that model on GPUs?

With large batches GPUs reuse each weight across many requests, reaching better cost per token, and a single GPU holds far more memory than one wafer.

Which of the three programming models is closest to CSL?

TT-Metalium: both use explicit, software-scheduled data movement between cores with private SRAM and no caches. CSL is finer-grained (words, not tiles) and has no DRAM behind it.
