# ch02 · depth review

Summary: Chapter 2 covers the right topics (reticle limit, the generation table, defects and spares, power, edge I/O), but it mostly lists facts. It almost never shows the arithmetic that links them. Five additions would turn it into a "how it behaves" chapter. (1) A "divide the wafer by its PEs" table that turns each headline spec into a per-PE, per-cycle number the learner will meet again in ch03–ch06. (2) A "marketed vs exposed cores" table across generations, which brings in the one hardware-measured data point the sources have (CS-1: 380k usable of 400k). (3) A real SDK benchmark result that tests the 20 PB/s claim. I ran `benchmarks/single-tile-matvec` in the 2.10.1 simulator for this review. (4) A quantitative model of the repair mechanism that shows why a naive one-spare-per-row scheme would fail. (5) The wafer described in time and in perimeter: hop latency across the wafer, and edge I/O against area. The accuracy review (A1–A18) and the pedagogy review (P1–P14) already cover the 850 MHz clock, the Poisson yield example, the corner waste, the meaning of sparse FLOPs and the cause of the memcpy margin. I do not repeat those. Where an item below builds on one of them, I say so.

## Gaps and additions (prioritised)

### D1 [critical] No "per-PE budget": headline specs are never broken down into what one PE gets per cycle
- **What is missing:** The table gives wafer totals (44 GB, 21 PB/s, 214 Pb/s, ~23 kW, ~15.6 PF dense). Nothing divides them by the PE count or by the clock. So the learner never sees that these totals are 900k copies of a very small machine. That is the core mental model for the rest of the course.
- **Why it matters:** Every later chapter works at the PE level (48 KB, 8 banks, 32-bit links, 1 FMA per cycle). Without this bridge the wafer totals stay abstract marketing numbers. With it, the learner can check the vendor totals, and they first meet the "per cycle" units that ch03–ch06 and the labs use. The table also shows that the numbers agree with each other, which is the best evidence the course has for them.
- **Source:** Vendor totals: `research_notes/.../wse_hardware.md` §1 Cited Findings (lines 13–31). The per-core derivations are in §1 Inferences (line 40: "21 PB/s ÷ 900k ≈ 23 GB/s per core … consistent with 8 single-cycle 32-bit banks"; line 39: 48.9 KB/47 KB per core). The 850 MHz clock is in line 21 and `benchmarks/conjugate-gradient/run.py:82`. The link rate is in §4 line 105 ("a 32-bit word per clock", arXiv:2209.15040). The 8-wide FP16 figure is in §3 / report Ch2 table.
- **Proposed addition:** A new `<h2>One wafer, 900,000 small machines</h2>` placed right after the generation table and the FLOPs callout. It holds this table, with every derived cell tagged `Derived` and the clock cell tagged `SDK 2.10.1`:

  | Wafer total (WSE-3) | ÷ 900k PEs | ÷ 0.85 GHz (SDK clock) | What it means later |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 46,225 mm² | 0.051 mm² per PE | — | matches the ~0.05 mm² core quoted in "Surviving defects": almost the whole square is core tiles (ch03) |
  | 44 GB SRAM | ~48.9 KB per PE (decimal GB) | — | the 48 KB per PE from ch03; 40 GB / 850k ≈ 47 KB on WSE-2 |
  | 21 PB/s memory BW | ~23 GB/s per PE | ~27 B/cycle ≈ 7 × 32-bit words | consistent with 8 single-cycle 32-bit banks (ch03) |
  | 214 Pb/s fabric BW | ~238 Gb/s per PE | ~35 B/cycle ≈ 9 × 32-bit words/cycle | ≈ 4 links × 2 directions × 32 bit, plus the ramp (ch04) |
  | ~15.6 PF dense FP16 (analyst) | ~17 GFLOP/s per PE | 8 FMAs = 16 FLOP/cycle | the SIMD width of the WSE-3 CE (ch03) |
  | ~23 kW system | ~25 mW per PE | — | a whole PE draws about as much as a small LED |

  Follow the table with one paragraph: "Read across a row and the marketing totals become one small machine repeated 900,000 times: 48 KB of memory that can serve about 7 words a cycle, roughly 9 words a cycle of mesh links, and an 8-wide FP16 unit. The rows agree with each other to within the rounding of the vendor figures, which is the best evidence we have that the totals are real. Note that 850 MHz is only the SDK benchmarks' assumption [SDK 2.10.1]; no official clock is published." Optional widget: a "generation" toggle (WSE-2 / WSE-3) that recomputes the table from 850k/40 GB/20 PB/s/220 Pb/s/4-wide versus 900k/44 GB/21/214/8-wide, plus a clock slider from 0.85 to 1.1 GHz, so the learner can see which rows depend on the unpublished clock.

### D2 [major] The 20 PB/s memory-bandwidth claim is never tested against anything the SDK can measure
- **What is missing:** The SDK ships a benchmark whose whole job is to report memory bandwidth and FLOPS per PE and scale them to the 750 × 994 CS-2 fabric: `benchmarks/single-tile-matvec`. The chapter quotes 20/21 PB/s as a vendor figure and never shows what a real CSL kernel gets.
- **Why it matters:** Objective 1 promises "vendor vs measured", and the pedagogy review (P7) notes there is no measured number in the chapter. This adds one, and it explains the gap mechanically. A fp32 matvec touches about 3 words per FMA (read A, read y, write y). That is about 12 B/cycle per PE, roughly 40% of the ~27 B/cycle the vendor total implies. The vendor peak assumes the full multi-bank, 16-bit SIMD path.
- **Source:** `/home/user/cerebras-sdk/extras/csl-extras-202604101435-6-d2f7d96e/examples/benchmarks/single-tile-matvec/{README.rst,run.py}`. `run.py` lines 199–225 define "absolute accesses" = 12·N² + 4·N bytes and FLOP = 2N² − N. Lines 265–280 use `secs = max_cycles / 850000000.0` and `scale_factor = (994*750)/(width*height)`. **Local simulator run for this review** (SDK 2.10.1, `--fabric-dims=8,3 --fabric-offsets=4,1 --params=width:1,height:1,tile_size:N`), with results:

  | arch | N | cycles | bytes touched | FLOP | B/cycle/PE | FLOP/cycle/PE | scaled to 750×994 @ 850 MHz |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | wse3 | 25 | 780 | 7,600 | 1,225 | 9.7 | 1.57 | 6.2 PB/s, 1.0 PF |
  | wse3 | 50 | 2,705 | 30,200 | 4,950 | 11.2 | 1.83 | 7.1 PB/s, 1.16 PF |
  | wse2 | 50 | 2,707 | 30,200 | 4,950 | 11.2 | 1.83 | 7.1 PB/s, 1.16 PF |
  | wse2 | 100 | 10,160 | 120,400 | 19,900 | 11.9 | 1.96 | 7.5 PB/s, 1.24 PF |
  | wse3 | 100 | 10,154 | 120,400 | 19,900 | 11.9 | 1.96 | 7.5 PB/s, 1.24 PF |

- **Proposed addition:** A callout titled "What a real kernel gets", placed after D1's table. Draft: "The SDK ships a benchmark that measures this. `single-tile-matvec` runs an N × N fp32 matrix-vector product on every PE and counts every SRAM byte it touches. In the 2.10.1 simulator, N = 100 takes 10,160 cycles and touches 120,400 bytes: about 12 B/cycle per PE, or about one fp32 multiply-add per cycle. Scaled to the 745,500-PE CS-2 fabric at 850 MHz, that is 7.5 PB/s and 1.24 PF fp32 [SDK 2.10.1, simulator] [Derived]. Two lessons follow. First, a fp32 FMA stream uses about 3 words per cycle, well short of the ~7 the vendor total implies, because the vendor peak assumes the full 16-bit SIMD path. Second, the per-PE rate does not change with N, because there is no cache to miss. N = 100 is close to the largest square fp32 matrix that fits in 48 KB (40 KB of A)." Tag the numbers `sdk` + `measured` (simulator). Optional lab hook for ch10/ch12: "Rerun with `cslc ./src/layout_matvec.csl --arch wse3 --fabric-dims=8,3 --fabric-offsets=4,1 --params=width:1,height:1,tile_size:100,iters:1 -o out --memcpy --channels=1; cs_python run.py --name out`."

### D3 [major] Marketed vs software-visible cores is shown for CS-2 only; the one hardware-measured data point (CS-1) is missing
- **What is missing:** The chapter gives 850k marketed against 745.5k fabric positions against ~737k program PEs for CS-2. It never puts the generations side by side. It also leaves out the SC20 paper (Cerebras/NETL authors, measured on hardware), which reports about 380,000 usable tiles of 400,000 on CS-1. And it does not use the area arithmetic, which bounds how much of the 850k could be spares.
- **Why it matters:** This is the closest the public record gets to the spare-core overhead the chapter says is "not published". A learner who sees 95% (CS-1) and ~88% (CS-2 fabric) can judge the "70,000 disabled cores" claim. The difference on CS-2 is 104,500, which is the same order of magnitude, so the claim is plausible as an upper bound but unverified. The learner also sees that the gap holds edge I/O and infrastructure as well as spares.
- **Source:** `wse_hardware.md` §1 line 14 (Rocki et al., SC20, arXiv:2010.03660: "about 380,000 usable tiles … BiCGStab … about 0.86 PFLOPS, roughly one third of peak"). Line 20 (750,994; 7/2 margin). Line 42 (745,500 vs 850k inference; arXiv:2209.15040 says "around 750,000 PEs"). Line 60 (70,000 claim, unverified).
- **Proposed addition:** A small table at the end of "Surviving defects", followed by one paragraph.

  | | Marketed cores | Software-visible | Fraction | Source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | CS-1 / WSE-1 | 400,000 | ~380,000 usable tiles | ~95% | SC20 paper `indep`-ish (Cerebras/NETL authors, measured) |
  | CS-2 / WSE-2 | 850,000 | 750 × 994 = 745,500 fabric positions; ≤ 743 × 992 ≈ 737k for a program | ~88% / ~87% | SDK 2.10.1, Derived |
  | CS-3 / WSE-3 | 900,000 | not published | — | — |

  Paragraph: "The hidden 5–13% includes spare cores and the infrastructure at the edge of the fabric. Cerebras does not publish the split. On CS-2 the gap is 104,500 cores, which makes the often-repeated '70,000 disabled cores' at least arithmetically possible, but it stays unverified. The same SC20 paper gives one of the few hardware-measured efficiency numbers: a BiCGStab solve on CS-1 sustained about 0.86 PF, roughly a third of peak [Indep/measured, Cerebras-NETL authors]." Also add an extra sentence to the A6 fix (0.05 vs 0.038 mm²): "0.05 mm² × 900k = 45,000 mm², 97% of the square; 0.038 mm² × 900k = 34,200 mm², 74%. ### D4 [major] The repair mechanism is drawn but not modelled: why "one spare per row" is not enough
- **What is missing:** The repair figure shows one bad core and a row shifting into one spare column. Pedagogy P4 adds the Poisson argument for why spares are needed. Neither explains how many spares, or how they must be arranged. The defects do not spread out neatly.
- **Why it matters:** This is the internal-behaviour question the topic raises. With 46 random defects spread over ~994 rows, the chance that two land in the same row is a birthday problem. One spare per full row fails about 65% of the time. So the real scheme must use finer segments, two-dimensional sparing, or spare links that allow shifts in both directions. This explains why the HC31 slides list both "redundant cores" and "redundant fabric links". It also shows why Cerebras calls the scheme "minimal spare capacity" rather than "no overhead".
- **Source:** Mechanism: `wse_hardware.md` §2 lines 55–56 (HC31 "redundant cores" + "redundant fabric links"; yield blog "small reserve of spare cores"). The 46 defects at 0.001/mm² are from line 57. The arithmetic is mine (Derived): P(no two of 46 defects share a repair segment) = ∏(1 − k/S) for k = 0..45.
  - S = 994 segments (one spare per full row): failure ≈ 65%
  - S = 1,988 (each row split in 2): ≈ 41%
  - S = 7,952 (8 segments per row): ≈ 12%
  - S = 11,928 (12 segments per row): ≈ 8%
- **Proposed addition:** (a) A short paragraph under the repair figure: "How many spares? Treat each row as a repair segment with one spare. With 46 random defects across ~994 rows, the chance that some row gets two defects is about 65% (the birthday problem), so a single spare per row would fail on most wafers. Cutting rows into 8 segments, each with its own spare, brings that to about 12% [Derived, toy model]. The real chip uses spare links as well as spare cores, so a repair can shift around a cluster. The exact scheme is not public." (b) Extend the existing defect simulator with a "spare segments per row" selector (1, 2, 4, 8, 12). After each roll, map the defects in the square onto a 994-row grid split into that many segments. Report "segments with ≥ 2 defects: k → repair fails / succeeds", and print the analytic failure probability from the product formula above next to it. This reuses the existing `defects` array, `half = 107.5` and the `#y-out` element, so no new data is needed.

### D5 [major] The wafer is never expressed in time or perimeter: hop latency across it, and edge I/O vs area
- **What is missing:** "Largest square" is given only as an area. The chapter never says the square is 215 mm on a side. It never says that crossing it takes about 1,744 hops corner to corner (750 + 994 positions), which is about 2 µs at one hop per cycle and 850 MHz. And it never says that I/O on the rim grows with the perimeter (4 × 215 = 860 mm) while compute grows with the area. The second point is the physical reason the host link is the narrowest pipe: about 112 Gb/s through SDK channels, 1.2 Tb/s for 12 × 100 GbE, against 214 Pb/s inside.
- **Why it matters:** The learner should leave ch02 knowing that the wafer is "big" in latency as well as area. That is why locality matters (ch04 and the stencil lab). They should also know that edge I/O cannot keep up with the area. Filling 44 GB through the SDK's 16 × 7 Gb/s channels takes about 3 s, which is why weights must stay resident or be streamed (ch07/ch08). Both follow directly from the geometry this chapter introduces.
- **Source:** Hop latency: report Ch5 line 62 and `wse_hardware.md` §4 line 105 ("latency of a hop is one clock cycle on average", arXiv:2209.15040). Clock: `wse_hardware.md` line 21 (SDK 850 MHz). I/O: §7 lines 166–171 (`benchmarks/bandwidth-test/README.rst`: ~7 Gb/s per channel, ≤ 16 channels, TCP ~200 µs; 12 × 100 GbE from a forum post; WSE-3T 2.4 Tb/s per wafer, vendor) and §7 Inferences line 174 (44 GB ≈ 3+ s). Side length: √46,225 = 215 mm, already in the chapter's JS (`half = 107.5`).
- **Proposed addition:** Two sentences for "From reticle to wafer": "The square is 215 mm on a side. At about one hop per cycle [Indep, arXiv:2209.15040] and the SDK's 850 MHz [SDK 2.10.1], a wavelet takes about 0.9 µs to cross the 750-column width of a CS-2 and about 2 µs to go corner to corner (1,744 hops) [Derived]. That is short for a whole chip, but a thousand times longer than one hop to a neighbour." Then expand "I/O lives on the rim" with: "The rim is 860 mm long while the area is 46,225 mm², so I/O grows with the perimeter and compute with the area. Inside, the fabric carries 214 Pb/s. Through the SDK's host channels, about 16 × 7 Gb/s ≈ 112 Gb/s [SDK 2.10.1] gets in, a ratio of about 2 million to one, and refilling all 44 GB that way would take about 3 s [Derived]. The CS-4 raises off-wafer I/O to 2.4 Tb/s per wafer [Vendor], still tiny next to the fabric." Optional figure: the existing wafer square with a highlighted 1,744-hop Manhattan path and a thin rim band labelled "I/O ∝ perimeter".

### D6 [minor] Power is given only as a system total; no density comparison and no per-PE figure
- **What is missing:** The chapter gives ~23 kW and stops. It does not say that this is about 0.5 W/mm² over the square. That figure is in the same range as a GPU die, so the hard problem is total current and uniform delivery, not hot spots. It also gives no per-PE power (~25 mW, which comes from D1).
- **Why it matters:** This complements pedagogy P13 (current at ~0.8 V ≈ 20 kA). Without a density figure the learner may think the wafer runs hotter than a GPU per mm². That is not what the numbers show, and the comparison explains why the solution is vertical delivery across the face rather than exotic cooling.
- **Source:** `wse_hardware.md` line 31 (CS-3 ~23 kW, vendor, a system figure that includes more than the wafer). Line 16 (CS-2 15 vs 20 kW conflict). The H100 TDP is not in the notes. If a fixer adds the GPU comparison, they must source H100 SXM ~700 W and ~814 mm² from `gpu_and_comparison.md` or tag it as an industry figure.
- **Proposed addition:** One sentence in "Power goes in through the face": "Spread over 46,225 mm², even the full ~23 kW system figure is under 0.5 W/mm², and the wafer itself draws less [Derived]: a power density in the same range as a GPU die. The difficulty is not hot spots. It is getting tens of thousands of amperes into every part of a 215 mm square evenly, which is why power comes in through the face." Add "~25 mW per PE" to D1's table.

### D7 [minor] Three ways past the reticle are not compared (stitching vs multi-die vs known-good-die tiles)
- **What is missing:** The chapter compares Cerebras only with the B200's two-die package. The research notes also hold the Theseus counterpoint: a known-good-die approach such as Tesla Dojo, where tested dies are assembled into a tile, may yield better than die stitching. The chapter never mentions it, so the yield story reads one-sided.
- **Why it matters:** Depth on a design topic includes the alternatives and their trade-offs. Stitching gives a uniform sub-1 mm fabric everywhere but must repair in place. Known-good-die avoids repair but needs die-to-die links at every boundary. The multi-die package (B200) needs one 10 TB/s link but only reaches 2 reticles.
- **Source:** `gpu_and_comparison.md` line 21 (B200: two reticle-limited dies, 10 TB/s NV-HBI, 208B transistors). `wse_hardware.md` §2 line 61 (Theseus, arXiv:2407.02079, counterpoint only). Line 62 (wires < 1 mm, uniform across die boundaries, STH HC34).
- **Proposed addition:** A three-row table at the end of "From reticle to wafer":

  | Approach | Example | Boundary crossing | Defect handling | Size limit |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Multi-die package | B200 | 10 TB/s die-to-die link [Vendor] | discard/bin each die before packaging | 2 reticles |
  | Known-good-die tiles | Tesla Dojo (per Theseus) | package-level links at every die edge | test dies first, use only good ones | tile/package size |
  | Wafer stitching | Cerebras WSE | on-wafer wires < 1 mm, same as within a field [Vendor] | spare cores + spare links, repaired in place | one wafer |

  Caption: "Theseus (arXiv:2407.02079) argues the known-good-die route may yield better; that is an academic argument, not a measurement." Do not add Dojo numbers. None are in the sources.

### D9 [minor] The simulator's GPU readout ignores the analytic expectation, so random rolls cannot be checked
- **What is missing:** The widget shows one random roll. It does not show the expected yield e^(−D·A), and it does not let the learner compare against Cerebras's 46-defect / 2.2 mm² model (2.2 / 46 ≈ 0.048 mm² per defect, which matches the 0.05 mm² core).
- **Why it matters:** Showing the formula next to the sample teaches the mechanism (Poisson yield) and the variance together. It also lets the learner reproduce the vendor model exactly. This builds on pedagogy P4, which proposes the formula as text only.
- **Source:** `wse_hardware.md` line 57 (0.001/mm², 46 defects, 2.2 mm²). The chapter's own JS (`dieW = 26, dieH = 32`; `deadWSE = hitsSq * 0.05`).
- **Proposed addition:** Append two lines to `#y-out`: `expected good-die fraction e^(−D·832 mm²) = ${(100*Math.exp(-d*dieW*dieH)).toFixed(0)}%` and `expected defects in square D·46,225 = ${(d*46225).toFixed(0)} → ${(d*46225*0.05).toFixed(1)} mm² of cores (Cerebras model at 0.001: 46 → 2.2 mm²)`. At the default slider value the second line reproduces the vendor model.
