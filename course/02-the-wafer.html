Chapter 2 · Part I · Why wafer-scale

One chip, one wafer

A normal chip is one small rectangle cut from a 300 mm silicon wafer. Cerebras keeps the whole wafer as one chip: 46,225 mm² Vendor, about 57× the area of an H100 die (~814 mm²) Derived. This chapter covers what that buys, what it costs, and how a chip that size survives manufacturing defects.

After this chapter you can

  • Say what keeping the wafer whole buys (on-silicon bandwidth, 44 GB of SRAM) and what it costs (unused corners, spare cores, power delivery, a narrow I/O rim).
  • Quote the WSE-2 and WSE-3 specs with the right caveats: sparse vs dense FLOPs, and vendor vs analyst vs measured numbers.
  • Divide a wafer total by its 900,000 PEs and get the per-PE, per-cycle budget the rest of the course uses.
  • Use the Poisson yield model to show why tiny cores plus spares are not optional.
  • Say why software sees a clean rectangle of PEs, how big it is on a CS-2, and why your program gets slightly less.

From reticle to wafer

Lithography exposes a wafer one reticle field at a time, and a field is at most about 26 × 33 mm Industry standard. That is why the largest conventional GPU dies stop near 800 mm², and why NVIDIA's B200 joins two such dies with a 10 TB/s die-to-die link Vendor. Cerebras prints an ordinary grid of reticle-sized fields, then adds wires across the scribe lines (the narrow strips between fields where a saw would normally cut). The on-chip network therefore continues from one field to the next as if the boundary were not there. Cerebras calls that network the fabric: a 2-D mesh linking every core to its four neighbours (Chapter 4). The fabric moves data in wavelets: 32-bit packets, each tagged with a colour that picks its route (Chapter 4). Each core is a processing element (PE); the course uses the two words interchangeably. A PE pairs a compute core (with its 48 KB of SRAM) with a five-port router: one port to each neighbour and one, the ramp, into its own core (Chapter 3, Chapter 4).

The result is a square about 215 mm on a side (√46,225 = 215) Derived, the largest that fits on the wafer. A full 215 mm square has a 304 mm diagonal, slightly more than the 300 mm wafer, so either the corners are trimmed or 46,225 mm² is rounded; the sources do not say Derived. What the wafer buys

Keeping the wafer whole buys one thing above all: data never has to leave the silicon. On a GPU cluster, every byte that moves between chips crosses a package edge, a SerDes and a cable; NVLink 4 gives an H100 about 0.9 TB/s (900 GB/s, both directions combined) Vendor (NVIDIA H100 datasheet; as in Chapter 17). On the wafer, wires between neighbouring cores are under 1 mm long Vendor (HC22: the Hot Chips 2022 talk by Sean Lie, held at the 34th Hot Chips and so sometimes filed as "HC34"; this page calls it HC22 throughout), and the fabric linking all of them carries 214 Pb/s ≈ 27 PB/s Vendor, about 30,000× one H100's NVLink Derived.

Area is the second gain. About half of each core's area is SRAM Vendor (HC22: 50:50 logic to SRAM), so the wafer holds 44 GB of single-cycle memory: the "capacity at that speed" from Chapter 1. A GPU cannot get that on one die, because one die stops at one reticle.

Two cautions. The WSE numbers are aggregates over 900,000 private memories and links, not one pipe any core can use at that rate. The fair on-chip GPU counterpart is its aggregate register-file and shared-memory bandwidth, tens to hundreds of TB/s (Chapter 1), which is not shown here. The point of the ladder is the off-chip links: the wafer replaces them with on-silicon wires.

The wafer is also big in time. On hardware a wavelet moves about one hop per cycle Indep (arXiv:2209.15040), and the times below use that figure. The SDK simulator you will use in the labs models 1.0 cycle per hop on WSE-2 but 2.0 on WSE-3 Measured (Chapter 4), so double the WSE-3 times there. At the WSE-2's 1.1 GHz Vendor (HC22), crossing the 750-column width of a CS-2 takes about 0.7 µs, and going corner to corner (750 + 994 = 1,744 hops) takes about 1.6 µs; at the 850 MHz the SDK benchmarks assume SDK 2.10.1, about 0.9 and 2 µs Derived. That is short for a whole machine, but about a thousand times longer than one hop to a neighbour, which is why the algorithms in later chapters favour locality. The rest of this chapter is the price of the bet.

Read the FLOPs carefully

WSE-3's 125 PF is a sparse FP16 figure; Cerebras has never published a dense one. A sparse figure counts the multiply-adds the chip skips because an operand is zero as if they had been done. Cerebras's dataflow cores skip zeros that are never sent (Chapter 3). Analysts get ~15.6 PF dense by assuming 8:1 unstructured sparsity Analyst: the headline counts 8 operations for every one actually performed, as if 7 of every 8 were zeros. (For WSE-2 the 10:1 ratio is Cerebras's own: the HC22 slide reads "75 PFLOPS FP16 Sparse / 7.5 PFLOPS FP16 Dense", sparse "with 10x sparsity acceleration" Vendor (HC22). Only WSE-3's 8:1 is an analyst assumption.) NVIDIA plays a milder version of the game. The H100's 1,979 TF BF16 is a "2:4 sparsity" number, a fixed pattern of 2 zeros in every 4 weights, worth exactly 2×; dense is ~989 TF Vendor.

A first-principles check: cores × SIMD width × 2 FLOPs × clock. WSE-2 is the one generation with an official clock, 1.1 GHz Vendor (HC22): 850k × 4 × 2 × 1.1 GHz ≈ 7.5 PF, exactly the HC22 dense figure Derived. No WSE-3 clock is published. If it also ran at 1.1 GHz, 900k × 8 × 2 × 1.1 GHz ≈ 15.8 PF, close to the analyst ~15.6 PF; at the 850 MHz the SDK benchmarks assume SDK 2.10.1, ≈ 12.2 PF Derived (WSE-3 clock unconfirmed). The WSE-2 → WSE-3 jump is mostly SIMD width (4 → 8) plus 6% more cores Derived.

Those are paper peaks. What the SDK simulator actually delivers for one f16 multiply-accumulate instruction (@fmach, memory to memory) is about 4 elements per cycle on both chips, when its two source arrays sit in opposite halves of the 16-byte SRAM bank cycle; placed badly, WSE-2 drops to about 2 Measured (Chapter 6). So WSE-2 reaches its 4-wide vendor width, but WSE-3's 8-wide path is not demonstrated in the SDK: 900k × 4 × 2 × 850 MHz ≈ 6.1 PF dense f16 is the measured-rate counterpart of the 12–16 PF above Derived. Read across a row and the marketing totals become one small machine repeated 900,000 times. At the SDK's 850 MHz on WSE-3, that is about 48 KB of memory that can serve about 7 words a cycle (27 B), roughly 9 words a cycle of mesh links (35 B), and an 8-wide FP16 unit Derived. The per-cycle cells are only as good as the clock: at WSE-2's published 1.1 GHz the same totals give about 21 B (5.3 words) of memory and 29 B of fabric per cycle Derived. Memory bandwidth stays under the roughly 32 B/cycle ceiling of 8 banks at either clock (Chapter 3 compares the two published bank models; the bank width is not public). The fabric row is a weaker check. A PE has 4 links, each carrying 4 B per cycle in each direction, so it sends and receives 4 × 2 × 4 B = 32 B/cycle, or 40 B with the ramp to its own core added. Vendor fabric totals sum these router ports over every PE, so each physical link is counted at both ends: do not multiply 32 B by 900k and compare it with real traffic, or you over-count by 2×. The WSE-3 fabric total lands inside 32–40 B only for clocks between about 740 and 930 MHz, and WSE-2's lands below 32 B at its own 1.1 GHz Derived; so the fabric check brackets a clock, it does not confirm one. Power agrees too: HC22 gives 30 mW peak per WSE-2 core Vendor, and 850k × 30 mW ≈ 25 kW is the same order as the 15–20 kW system figures Derived. That rough agreement is the best evidence we have that the totals are real.

What a real kernel gets

The SDK ships a benchmark that measures this: single-tile-matvec runs an N × N fp32 matrix-vector product on each PE and counts every SRAM byte it touches: 12N² + 4N bytes, because each multiply-add reads A[i,j] and y[i] and writes y[i] back (3 × 4 B), while x[j] is read once per column (the 4N) SDK 2.10.1 (run.py). In the 2.10.1 simulator, N = 100 on one WSE-3 PE took 10,154 cycles and touched 120,400 bytes: about 12 B/cycle, and about one fp32 multiply-add per cycle Measured (SDK 2.10.1 simulator, --arch wse3, 8 × 3 fabric, a 1 × 1 program rectangle, tile_size:100). The benchmark's own scaling to 750 × 994 = 745,500 PEs (the CS-2 size in its README; see the note on that size) at 850 MHz gives 7.5 PB/s and 1.24 PF fp32 SDK 2.10.1 Derived. WSE-2 gives the same within a few cycles (10,160).

Two lessons. First, 12 B per cycle is 3 words: one multiply-add per cycle times the 3 words each one touches. That is about 40% of the ~7 words the WSE-3 vendor total implies at 850 MHz, or about 56% of the 5.3 words at 1.1 GHz Derived. The vendor figure matches the measured f16 rate instead: @fmach at about 4 elements per cycle touches 3 operands × 4 × 2 B = 24 B ≈ 6 words per cycle, close to the per-PE vendor memory figure (27 B/cycle on WSE-3 at 850 MHz, 21 B on WSE-2 at 1.1 GHz) (Chapter 6) Derived. A full 8-wide f16 multiply-accumulate from memory would need 48 B/cycle, more than the vendor total supplies, which is one reason the 8-wide peak is not reached memory to memory. Second, the per-PE rate does not change with N, because there is no cache to miss (Chapter 3). N = 100 is close to the largest square fp32 matrix that fits in 48 KB (40 KB of A).

Surviving defects

Every wafer has defects. Cerebras's own model assumes about 46 on the wafer-scale square Vendor modelling; real defect densities are not published. On a GPU wafer a defect landing in a die usually kills or downgrades that die, and the bigger the die, the more silicon goes with it. GPUs already soften this with spares: an H100 SXM enables 132 SMs Vendor out of the 144 in the full GH100 design Vendor (NVIDIA Hopper architecture whitepaper), so a defect in one SM costs that SM, not the die. An SM (streaming multiprocessor) is the GPU's core unit (Chapter 17).

A wafer-scale chip cannot throw itself away, so Cerebras pushes the same idea about 100× finer. A WSE-3 core is about 0.05 mm², roughly 1/100th of an H100 SM Vendor (yield blog); the HC22 deck gives the WSE-2 core as 228 × 170 µm = 0.039 mm² Vendor (HC22). Either way it is tiny. (0.05 mm² × 900k = 45,000 mm², 97% of the square; 0.039 mm² × 850k = 33,000 mm², 71%. The two may count different things, such as the core with or without its router, or simply differ by generation Derived.) Spare cores and spare fabric links are built in, and the fabric is reconfigured to route around bad cores Vendor. When and how that configuration is done is not public.

Worked example: why spares are not optional

With randomly placed point defects at density D, an area A is defect-free with probability e−D·A (the Poisson yield model). Take Cerebras's own 0.001 defects/mm². A full-reticle 26 × 33 mm die (858 mm²) is defect-free with probability e−0.858 ≈ 42%. A 46,225 mm² wafer chip with no redundancy is defect-free with probability e−46 ≈ 10−20: you would never ship one. With 0.05 mm² cores, the same 46 defects disable about 46 of 900,000 cores (0.005%), and a modest pool of spares absorbs them Derived (simple Poisson model).

How many spares, and where? Treat each row as a repair segment with one spare. With 46 random defects across ~994 rows, the chance that some row gets two of them is about 65% (the birthday problem), so a single spare per row would fail on most wafers. Cutting each row into 8 segments, each with its own spare, brings the failure rate down to about 12% Derived (toy model). This is why the Hot Chips 2019 slides list both "redundant cores" and "redundant fabric links" Vendor: spare links let a repair shift around a cluster. Cerebras describes the result as "high yield with minimal spare capacity"; the real scheme is not public. Try it in the simulator below.

Cerebras's own model at 0.001 defects/mm² has the wafer taking 46 defects and losing only about 2.2 mm² of silicon (2.2 ÷ 46 ≈ 0.048 mm² per defect, one core each), which it presents as a "164× reduction in dead silicon" versus a GPU Vendor modelling. That multiplier only works if the GPU loses about one SM per defect; if it discarded whole dies the ratio would be in the thousands. Switch the simulator's accounting to see both. Spare-core ratios are not published; a widely repeated claim of "70,000 disabled cores" traces only to a blog post and is unverified.

The comparison leaves out two fixed costs. The square uses only about 65% of the round wafer, so about 24,000 mm² of corner silicon is never used, defect or not Derived. And the spare cores and links take up area on every wafer, defective or not. There is also no binning: every wafer has to work. Cerebras trades a small, predictable overhead for having no yield lottery at all.

Power, cooling and the edges

Power goes in through the face

At core voltages below 1 V, 15–20 kW into the wafer means on the order of 20,000 amperes Derived (assuming ~0.8 V; the voltage is not published). Pushed in from the edge across 215 mm of thin metal, that current would lose much of its voltage on the way. So power is delivered vertically through the face of the wafer, and a closed water loop (the CS-3 "engine block") removes the heat. A custom connector lets the wafer expand thermally without cracking Secondary sources.

The density is not the problem. Spread over 46,225 mm², even the full ~23 kW system figure is about 0.5 W/mm², and the wafer itself draws less Derived. That is below a high-end GPU die (an H100 SXM at up to 700 W Vendor (H100 datasheet, max TDP) over ~814 mm² Vendor is ~0.9 W/mm² Derived). The hard part is getting tens of thousands of amperes evenly into every part of a 215 mm square. The CS-4 puts AC/DC conversion about 0.5 mm from the wafer, versus about 50 mm on GPU boards Vendor, which shortens the path that huge current travels.

I/O lives on the rim

Data enters and leaves through I/O channels at the edge of the fabric Derived (physical layout not public). The rim is 860 mm long while the area is 46,225 mm², so I/O grows with the perimeter and compute with the area. Inside, the fabric carries 214 Pb/s. Two numbers describe the door. The system I/O of a CS-2 or CS-3 is 12 × 100 GbE = 1.2 Tb/s Vendor (Cerebras system page; PSC Neocortex CS-3 specifications), and the CS-4 doubles it to 2.4 Tb/s per wafer Vendor. What one SDK program gets through the memcpy path is smaller: the SDK's bandwidth-test says the host connects "via 100Gbps ethernets" and one I/O channel tops out "around 7Gbps", with at most 16 channels, so about 16 × 7 Gb/s ≈ 112 Gb/s SDK 2.10.1. The fabric is about 180,000× the system I/O and about 2 million × the memcpy path. Refilling all 44 GB would take about 0.3 s at the 1.2 Tb/s system rate and about 3 s through the memcpy path Derived. Either is an eternity next to the fabric, which is why weights stay resident on the wafer (Chapter 7, Chapter 8). A model larger than 44 GB cannot stay resident on one wafer: for training, Cerebras streams the weights in from external memory one layer at a time (MemoryX, Chapter 8), and that is when the off-wafer bandwidth figure matters.

There is a software consequence too, which Chapter 7 covers. To move data from the edge to your PEs, the SDK's memcpy runtime compiles its own small programs onto ordinary PEs that frame your rectangle: columns on the west and east, plus a row above and below. Those PEs are not available to you. On a CS-2 your rectangle must therefore be at least 7 columns narrower and 2 rows shorter than the 750 × 994 fabric: at most 743 × 992 ≈ 737k PEs, against 850k marketed SDK 2.10.1 Derived (if 750 × 994 is in fact the program size, the fabric is 757 × 996; see the note above). The WSE-3 equivalent of 750 × 994 is not published in the SDK or anywhere we could find; expect the same kind of gap below 900k.

Check yourself

What does keeping the wafer whole buy, in one sentence?

Traffic between cores never leaves the silicon: sub-millimetre wires carry ~27 PB/s instead of off-chip links like NVLink (~0.9 TB/s), and the area holds 44 GB of single-cycle SRAM.

Why is a small core size essential to wafer-scale yield?

A defect only kills the unit it lands in. With ~0.05 mm² cores and spares, each defect costs a tiny area instead of a whole ~800 mm² die.

At 0.001 defects/mm², what fraction of 858 mm² GPU dies are defect-free, and what is the chance a 46,225 mm² chip with no spares is?

e−0.858 ≈ 42%; e−46 ≈ 10−20. Without fine-grained spares a wafer-scale chip would never yield. Even with spares, one spare per row would fail on most wafers (birthday problem), so the spares must be finer-grained.

A slide says "WSE-3: 125 PFLOPS". What should you ask?

Sparse or dense, and at what precision? 125 PF is sparse FP16; dense is not published (analysts estimate ~15.6 PF). The 125 / 15.6 ≈ 8× ratio tells you how much zero-skipping the headline assumes.

Estimate WSE-3 dense FP16 peak from first principles. Which input is weakest?

900k PEs × 8 FMAs × 2 FLOPs × clock: ≈ 12.2 PF at the SDK's 850 MHz, ≈ 15.8 PF at WSE-2's official 1.1 GHz. The clock is the weak input, because no WSE-3 clock is published. The same arithmetic for WSE-2 at its published 1.1 GHz gives 7.5 PF, matching HC22's dense figure. The SIMD width is the second weak input: the SDK simulator's f16 @fmach reaches about 4 elements per cycle, not 8, which gives ≈ 6.1 PF at 850 MHz (Chapter 6).

Why can't your program use all 850,000 WSE-2 cores?

The SDK exposes only 750 × 994 = 745,500 positions. The rest are most likely spares and edge infrastructure; Cerebras has not published the breakdown. The memcpy runtime then runs on PEs that frame your program, so your rectangle must be at least 7 columns and 2 rows smaller: at most 743 × 992 ≈ 737k. (One caveat: the same SDK benchmark also uses 750 × 994 as a program size, which would put the fabric at 757 × 996; see the note.)
