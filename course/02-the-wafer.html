Chapter 2 · Part I · Why wafer-scale

One chip, one wafer

A normal chip is one small rectangle cut from a 300 mm silicon wafer. Cerebras keeps the whole wafer as one chip: 46,225 mm², about 57× the area of an H100 die. This chapter covers what that buys, what it costs, and how a chip that size survives manufacturing defects.

After this chapter you can

  • Quote the WSE-2 and WSE-3 specs with the right caveats (sparse vs dense FLOPs, vendor vs measured).
  • Explain how small cores plus redundancy turn "every wafer has defects" into a usable chip.
  • Say why software sees a clean rectangle of PEs, and how big that rectangle is.

From reticle to wafer

Lithography exposes a wafer one reticle field at a time — at most about 26 × 33 mm. That is why the largest conventional GPU dies stop near 800 mm², and why NVIDIA's B200 glues two such dies together with a 10 TB/s link. Cerebras prints an ordinary grid of reticle-sized fields, then adds wires across the scribe lines between them so that the on-chip network continues seamlessly from one field to the next. The result is the largest square that fits in the wafer, wired as one chip.

Read the FLOPs carefully WSE-3's 125 PF is a sparse FP16 figure; Cerebras has never published a dense one. A sanity check agrees with the analyst estimate: 900k cores × 8 FP16 FMAs (16 FLOPs) × ~1 GHz ≈ 14–15 PF dense Derived; clock unconfirmed. The WSE-2 → WSE-3 jump is mostly SIMD width (4 → 8) plus 6% more cores. NVIDIA plays the same game: the H100's 1,979 TF BF16 is a 2:4-sparsity number; dense is ~989 TF.

Surviving defects

Every wafer has defects — a few dozen point flaws is normal. On a GPU wafer a defect landing in a die usually kills or downgrades that die, and the bigger the die, the more silicon goes with it. A wafer-scale chip cannot throw itself away, so Cerebras makes the unit of failure tiny: a core is about 0.05 mm², roughly 1/100th of an H100 SM. Spare cores and spare fabric links are built in, and the fabric is configured at manufacturing time to route around bad cores.

Cerebras's own model at 0.001 defects/mm² has the wafer taking 46 defects and losing only about 2.2 mm² of silicon, which it presents as a "164× reduction in dead silicon" versus a GPU Vendor modelling. The simulator above makes the same point with your own numbers. Spare-core ratios are not published; a widely repeated claim of "70,000 disabled cores" traces only to a blog post and is unverified.

Power, cooling and the edges

Power goes in through the face

A ~23 kW system cannot feed a wafer from its edges. Power is delivered vertically through the face of the wafer, and a closed water loop (the CS-3 "engine block") removes the heat. A custom connector lets the wafer expand thermally without cracking. The CS-4 puts AC/DC conversion about 0.5 mm from the wafer, versus about 50 mm on GPU boards Vendor.

I/O lives on the rim

Data enters and leaves at the wafer edges. That has a software consequence you will meet in Chapter 7: the runtime reserves columns on the west and east sides of your program for host I/O, so on a CS-2 your rectangle of PEs must be 7 columns narrower and 2 rows shorter than the 750 × 994 fabric — about 737k usable PEs out of 850k marketed SDK 2.10.1 Derived.

Check yourself

Why is a small core size essential to wafer-scale yield?

A defect only kills the unit it lands in. With ~0.05 mm² cores and spares, each defect costs a tiny area instead of a whole ~800 mm² die.

A slide says "WSE-3: 125 PFLOPS". What should you ask?

Sparse or dense, and at what precision? 125 PF is sparse FP16; dense is not published (analysts estimate ~15.6 PF).

Why can't your program use all 850,000 WSE-2 cores?

Spares are hidden by the repair logic, and the I/O runtime reserves columns around your program. The SDK exposes a 750 × 994 fabric; your rectangle must be 7 columns and 2 rows smaller.
