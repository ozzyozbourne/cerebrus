Chapter 9 · Part II · The machine

From model to tokens: compiling and serving on wafers

Chapter 8 showed the two ways Cerebras gets past the 44 GB of SRAM on one wafer: stream the weights through it, or pin them across several wafers. This chapter follows a model the rest of the way: how a compiler turns its graph into rectangles of PEs, how pinned layers become a decode pipeline, what one token's ~0.5 ms is plausibly made of, and why the same four CS-3s went from 450 to 2,100 tokens per second.

After this chapter you can

  • Describe the compile path (extract → match → size, place and route → link) and explain why placing kernels is a floorplanning problem whose slowest rectangle sets the throughput.
  • Contrast the two compile targets (weight streaming and the layer pipeline), and explain how an inference wafer runs a model as a pipeline of regions, where the KV cache lives, and why per-user speed and total throughput are different numbers.
  • Build a per-token decode latency budget from this course's measured constants, say which parts are derived and which are guessed, and check it against the 476 µs per token implied by the published 2,100 tok/s.
  • Compute the expected speedup of speculative decoding from the acceptance rate, and explain why a layer pipeline makes verification cheap for one user, why the draft model is not cheap on a wafer, and when speculation costs the system throughput.
  • Explain what mixture-of-experts, 4-bit weights, disaggregated prefill and sparsity do on a wafer, and attribute each jump in the speed history to a mechanism, where the sources allow.
What is public, and what is not Cerebras's ML compiler and its production inference kernels are closed. The primary sources are a few talks and blogs: the ISPD 2020 placement contest slides, Hot Chips 2022 and 2024, the weight-streaming white paper, and product blogs. Everything below that goes beyond them is labelled Derived (arithmetic on published or measured numbers) or Unverified. The decode budget in particular is a model built from this course's own simulator measurements, not a description of Cerebras's kernels.

The compile path: from a graph to rectangles

Cerebras's clearest public account of its compiler is in the slides it presented when it set the ISPD 2020 placement contest: a public challenge, run with the International Symposium on Physical Design, to place neural networks on the CS-1 Vendor (ISPD 2020 contest closing session). They describe a four-stage stack:

  1. Extract. "Convert TF via XLA to Cerebras graph IR." The model becomes a graph of operations.
  2. Match. "Obtain graph covering of Cerebras graph with Cerebras kernels." Each group of operations is replaced by a hand-written kernel from a library. A kernel is a parameterised spatial program, much like the CSL programs of Part III.
  3. Place & route. "Assign kernels to WSE regions and connect them up." Each kernel gets a rectangle of PEs, and the compiler picks fabric routes (Chapter 4's colors) between rectangles.
  4. Link. "Create executable output."

Step 3 is the interesting one, because the wafer gives the compiler "freedom to pick and choose size (# cores) and location" for every kernel Vendor. The slides frame it as a global optimisation. The variables are the area and location of each kernel. The constraints are to fit compute within the cores, fit memory within the SRAMs, and reduce communication. The objectives are to "maximize throughput, utilization" and "minimize latency". And it must "compile in seconds to compete with CPU/GPUs".

One layer, many shapes

Each layer is "a program with nested loops", and parallelising it means mapping some of those loops onto the 2-D mesh. The contest gave teams the cost model for a convolution kernel. Its free variables are the splits h′, w′, c′, k′: how many ways the image height, width, input channels and output channels are cut Vendor. It returns the rectangle's shape, its memory per core, and Δt: the cycles the kernel needs per sample, which is this kernel's stage time in the pipeline.

convperf(H, W, R, S, C, K; h', w', c', k'):
  height = h'·w'·(k'+1)                     rectangle rows
  width  = 3·c'                             rectangle columns
  time   = ⌈H/h'⌉·⌈W/w'⌉·⌈C/c'⌉·⌈K/k'⌉·R·S    Δt, cycles per sample
  memory = (C/c')·(K/k')·R·S + ((W+S−1)/w')·((H+R−1)/h')·(K/k')   per core

Double the splits and the rectangle gets bigger, Δt halves and the per-core memory roughly halves: the slide's example takes a 6×6 split to 6×12 for "½ Δt" and "~½ memory". The whole network is a pipeline of these rectangles, and "overall Δt is the max of the layer's Δt": the slowest rectangle sets the rate for everything. Two more costs come from the joins. Wire length approximates fabric congestion between layers ("L1 distance between layers"). An adapter is needed when one layer's output split does not match the next layer's input split (h = 6 feeding h = 12, say), and it costs extra area. The contest score was a weighted sum of pipeline time, wire length, adapter cost and optimizer runtime, subject to the memory limit, non-overlapping rectangles and the total wafer size. Cerebras called it "a simplified version of our commercial objective function" Vendor.

The contest had 20 benchmark graphs (ResNet-like vision models, U-Net-like scientific ones, language models), each scored at two weightings. A CUHK team, RippleWafer, won. Their lesson was that "conventional floorplanning methods may not be suitable" and that "how to determine best kernel shapes efficiently is the key". Their final method searched for the smallest achievable Δt, generated the legal shapes that met it for each kernel, then placed them in rows that follow the graph's topology. On one benchmark that cut wire length from 2,080 to 562, against simulated annealing, for an 8% worse Δt Vendor, team slide. Follow-up work, CU.POKer, was published in IEEE TCAD (abstract) Indep.

Sized for

Two things carry over to everything later in the chapter. First, balance: a pipeline is only as fast as its slowest stage, so the compiler spends area where the work is. Second, sizing by memory: switch the widget to "pinned inference" and each layer's weights must fit in its own rectangle's SRAM. The minimum widths then use most of the wafer, and little freedom is left to balance. Hot Chips 2024 says the same of inference regions: their size is "determined based on memory and compute requirements" Vendor.

Two compile targets, one graph

The 2020 stack compiled for the layer-pipelined mode of Chapter 8: every layer resident at once, each in its own rectangle. Weight streaming (2021) changed the target: "the compiler maps one kernel (layer) at a time onto the entire WSE, first for the forward pass and then for the backward pass" Vendor (training docs). Pinned-weight inference (2024) went back to rectangles, now sized to hold weights and KV cache.

The inference pipeline

Inference flips the trade. Decode is bandwidth-bound (Chapter 1), so the winning move is to keep every weight in SRAM and never fetch it: "we don't need to access external memory to load parameters because they're already there" Vendor. The cost is capacity. Besides the weights, decode keeps a KV cache (Chapter 1): the attention keys and values of every earlier token, one copy per sequence, growing with context length. On a GPU it lives in HBM. On Cerebras it must fit in whatever SRAM the weights leave free. Llama 3.1-70B needs about 140 GB at 16-bit Derived, so at launch in August 2024 its 80 layers were spread across four CS-3 systems, each holding a contiguous block of 20 layers, with only activations passing between wafers Vendor (Hot Chips 2024).

Hot Chips 2024 describes the mapping directly Vendor:

  • The opposite of a GPU. "GPU: multiple chips to run a single layer. Cerebras: fraction of a chip to run a single layer."
  • Regions. "Model layers are mapped to wafer regions", with "model weights and KV cache stored locally in the region memory close to the compute".
  • One token per stage. "Each wafer region processes 1 token": a matrix × vector at local batch 1. "Regions are placed to reduce latency": the next region is "physically adjacent", and the "last region output [is] cycled back to generate next token". That loop is the autoregressive decode of Chapter 1.
  • Many users fill the pipe. "Single user is using only a fraction of the total memory bandwidth", so other users' tokens occupy the other stages at the same moment, and "every user gets full performance".
  • Prefill. "Prompt tokens can be processed in parallel": several of one user's prompt tokens are in the pipe at once, one stage behind another.
  • Across wafers. "Keep almost all high communication on wafer… Transfer only activations between wafers", over CS-3's "low latency RDMA over Ethernet". Llama 3.1-8B (16 GB) fits on one wafer.

Pipeline decode: one user versus many

Users in flight

This is pipeline parallelism. A single sequence's token visits the regions in order, so at any moment only one region of one wafer is working on it. The rest are kept busy only when other sequences are in flight. Adding users raises total throughput without slowing anyone, until the pipe is full or the KV cache runs out of SRAM. At 2,100 tok/s, the time per output token is about 476 µs Derived. Since October 2024 that speed includes speculative decoding (below), so one trip through all four wafers may take longer than 476 µs and yield more than one token. The even 20-layer split is shown on the Hot Chips 2024 slide Vendor; ~35 GB of weights per wafer follows from it Derived, though the embedding and output layers make the real split slightly uneven.

Worked example: the inter-wafer budget
  • Per token, the data crossing each wafer boundary is one activation vector: Llama-70B's hidden size of 8,192 Vendor (Meta's model config) × 2 B ≈ 16 KB Derived.
  • HC2024: "CS-3 IO is <5us; 4 hops required; <1% impact to latency" Vendor. Presumably the four hops are three wafer-to-wafer hops plus the return trip to wafer 1 Derived.
  • At 450 tok/s (launch), one token takes 2.2 ms. Four hops at 5 µs add 20 µs, about 0.9%. At 2,100 tok/s the same 20 µs is about 4% Derived. That is one reason CS-4 cuts the hop to "as low as 2 microseconds" Vendor.
  • Bandwidth: HC2024 budgets 100 Gb/s of CS-3's 1.2 Tb/s, "<10% of available" Vendor. One user at 2,100 tok/s moves only about 34 MB/s per boundary Derived; the headroom is for many users and prefill.

KV-cache arithmetic: what limits users and context

For Llama-3-70B (80 layers, 8 KV heads of width 128 Vendor, Meta config), the KV cache per token is 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 (K and V) × 2 B = 327,680 B ≈ 0.33 MB Derived, the same figure as Chapter 1. Four wafers hold 176 GB. Take away 140 GB of weights and ~36 GB is left. Even if all of it held KV and none held activations or code, that is about 110,000 tokens of context in total across all users: at most ~13 users at 8K context, or less than one user at 128K Derived. That is an upper bound. Every PE also holds its code and task table, ~4.6 KB on WSE-3 even for a minimal CSL program (Chapter 10) Measured. Across 3.6 million PEs that is ~16.6 GB, which leaves ~19 GB: ~59,000 tokens, or ~7 users at 8K, and fewer once activation and partial-sum buffers take their share Derived. On the GPU side the same KV cache sits in HBM next to the weights. This is why Hot Chips 2024 lists "KV cache optimizations" among the next techniques Vendor, and why long-context deployments such as Llama 3.1-405B at 128K need many wafers.

How many wafers, and how fast can it possibly decode?

Weights and KV cache pinned in SRAM (44 GB per WSE-3), compared with the HBM of H100s and B200s. KV assumes Llama-3 shapes (8 KV heads × 128, stored at 16-bit). Layer counts for sizes between 8B, 70B and 405B are interpolated (and extrapolated above 405B), so treat those as illustrative. The 120B and 1T presets are dense models of that size with Llama-3 shapes. The real models of those sizes (gpt-oss-120B, Kimi K2.6) are mixture-of-experts, with far fewer parameters active per token, other layer counts and other KV formats, so these bounds are not theirs (see the MoE section). The bound is bandwidth ÷ bytes read per decode step (all weights plus every user's KV). For GPUs it assumes tensor parallelism, with every device working on every layer. For the wafer it follows the region mapping, where one user's token is in one layer region at a time, so it divides the aggregate by the number of layers. Model

A decode latency budget

Where we are. A model's layers are pinned as regions across four wafers, one token visits them in order, and the KV cache must fit in the SRAM the weights leave free. Now: how long does one token's trip take, and where does the time go?

Chapter 8 ended with "the sources do not break this down". They still do not, but this course has measured most of the ingredients in the simulator. Below is a bounded estimate of one token's trip through Llama 3.1-70B on four CS-3s. It is built from those constants and a simple, explicit model of the mapping. Treat it as an engineering estimate, not a reverse-engineering of Cerebras's kernels. Some constants come from labs later in the course; where that happens, the text says where, and for now you can take them as given.

The model

  1. Regions. One region per layer, 20 per wafer, so ~45,000 PEs per region, about 212 × 212 Derived. Every PE in a region holds a slice of all of that layer's matrices (Q, K, V, O, and the three FFN matrices) and joins in each matrix-vector product in turn. This follows HC2024's "fraction of a chip to run a single layer" and Hot Chips 2022's GEMM mechanism (Chapter 8) Unverified mapping.
  2. Weights per PE. 140 GB ÷ (4 × 900,000 PEs) ≈ 38.9 KB per PE: 81% of each 48 KB SRAM, and 88% of the ~44 KB left once code and the task table take their ~4.6 KB (Chapter 10) Derived. That leaves ~9 KB per PE raw, or ~4.5 KB after code, for KV cache and buffers, which is why the KV figures above are upper bounds.
  3. GEMV time per layer. Each PE streams its 38.9 KB of weights once per token, at the measured kernel rate below (or at the faster rate a full-width kernel might reach).
  4. Collectives per layer. Each of the layer's four sequential matrix-vector stages (QKV, O, gate/up, down) needs the input vector broadcast across the region and partial sums reduced across it: at least 8 collectives. A real layer adds more: two RMSNorm reductions (sum of squares, then a broadcast of the scale), the softmax maximum and sum across the region's slices of the KV cache, and the hand-off of the activation to the next region, so ~12–14 is more realistic. Each is latency-bound. The default of ~212 hops per collective assumes it runs along one dimension of the region or from a central root; a broadcast from a corner across both dimensions is ~424 hops. So the default is optimistic. The cycles per hop are measured: 2.0 for a pure router hop on WSE-3 (Chapter 4), and 9.2 when every hop passes through a PE's receive-add-send, as in a chain reduce, measured in Lab 2 (Chapter 12) Measured.
  5. Attention. Reading the user's KV cache for this layer: 4,096 B per context token (8 heads × 128 × K and V × 2 B), spread over the region's PEs, at the same streaming rate Derived.
  6. Hops. Four wafer hops at <5 µs (CS-3) Vendor.

The f32 result reproduces Chapter 8's benchmark run (10,160 cycles). In f16, WSE-3 streams weights about twice as fast, ~4 f16 multiply-accumulates per cycle, the rate Chapter 6 measured for @fmach, while WSE-2 gains nothing. The f16 N = 140 tile is 39.2 KB, almost exactly the 38.9 KB per PE above, so one layer's weights take ~5,200 cycles to stream with this kernel, 6.1 µs at the SDK's assumed 850 MHz Derived. No WSE-3 clock is published; WSE-2 runs at 1.1 GHz (HC22) Vendor.

This is the kernel's limit, not the chip's. HC2024 gives the WSE-3 core as "8-way SIMD for 16b data" with "full bandwidth for full SIMD performance" (Chapter 3) Vendor, twice what this kernel reaches. A plausible reason Derived: the kernel is an AXPY, so every f16 multiply-accumulate reads a weight (2 B) and an accumulator element (2 B) from SRAM, then writes the accumulator back. If WSE-3 reads 2 × 64 bits per cycle, as HC2022 gives for WSE-2's datapath, that is 16 B of reads ÷ 4 B = 4 multiply-accumulates per cycle, 8 B/cycle of weights, and the measured 7.5 is 94% of it. Weights can only ever use the read bandwidth (the write port carries results). A kernel that kept its partial sums out of SRAM, in registers or in a dot-product form, would read only weights and could in principle reach 8 per cycle: ~15 B/cycle at the same efficiency, ~2,600 cycles per layer. Whether WSE-3 hardware or Cerebras's production kernels do so is not public Unverified, and all of these rates are simulator figures.

Now the budget. 2,100 tok/s means 476 µs per output token, or 5.95 µs per layer over 80 layers, which is 5,060 cycles at 850 MHz Derived. The measured GEMV alone takes ~5,200 cycles. So on this model, streaming a region's own weights is not a rounding error. It is the biggest single term: between about half (an 8-wide kernel) and all (the measured kernel) of the per-layer budget, before any reduction, attention or hop. The model can still fit the published speed in three ways: a WSE-3 clock above 850 MHz; production kernels that beat this naive one (by up to ~2× if they reach the full SIMD width); or speculative decoding, which spreads one pass over more than one output token. The sources do not say which of these is true.

What-if

Things to try. What-if A is the launch: the measured kernel, collectives that hop through each PE's compute at 9.2 cycles (~15,600 cycles per layer), and no speculation. The token then takes about 2 ms, ~500 tok/s, near the 450 tok/s of August 2024. What-if B pulls three levers at once: a kernel at the full 8-wide rate, collectives at router speed, and speculation with k = 4, α = 0.8 and a draft priced as it would be on a wafer (c = 0.35, explained below). It lands at ~430 µs per output token, ~2,300 tok/s, just above the published 2,100–2,314 tok/s. Take any lever away and it falls short: at the measured kernel rate B gives ~610 µs (~1,600 tok/s), and without speculation ~590 µs (~1,700 tok/s). Cerebras's October 2024 blog credits "re-written or optimized" MatMul and "reduce/broadcast" kernels, asynchronous wafer I/O and speculative decoding Vendor. The budget shows those are the right kind of changes to give a 4.7× gain on the same silicon. It does not show that these were the actual numbers. Two more lessons:

  • Hops between wafers are small but not free. Twenty microseconds is 4% of 476 µs. At CS-4's claimed >4,400 tok/s per user on gpt-oss-120B (227 µs per token), four 5 µs hops would be ~9% of a token, which is why the 2 µs hop matters Derived.
  • Context length shows up late. At 8K tokens, attention adds ~100 cycles per layer at the measured kernel rate. At 128K it adds ~1,600 cycles per layer, about half of router-speed collectives (and a tenth of what-if A's), and the KV no longer fits in four wafers anyway.
Two ceilings, not one: a correction to the bandwidth argument
  • Pipe-full ceiling. If every region is busy with a different user, the system reads all 140 GB of weights per "round" at the aggregate rate. Four WSE-3s give 84 PB/s ÷ 140 GB ≈ 600,000 tokens/s in total across users at the headline bandwidth Derived. The headline counts every SRAM port, reads and writes; a GEMV can stream weights through the read ports only, so even this overstates what weights can use.
  • One-user ceiling. A single user's token is in one region at a time, so it sees only 1/80 of that bandwidth: ~7,500 tok/s at the headline 21 PB/s, an overstatement for the same reason. For the weights alone, at a region's GEMV rate, it is ~2,000 tok/s at the measured kernel rate (7.5 B/cycle, 850 MHz) and ~4,000 tok/s at the full 8-wide SIMD (15 B/cycle) Derived. HC2024 says it in words: "single user is using only a fraction of the total memory bandwidth" Vendor.
  • It is a dependency bound. Layer l + 1 cannot start until layer l has finished, so one token's time is at least the sum over layers of each region's GEMV time, however much bandwidth the rest of the wafer has. Only a faster region (clock, kernel rate), fewer weights per token (MoE) or more tokens per pass (speculation) moves it.
  • So "decode on the wafer is not bandwidth-bound" is true of the aggregate. For one user, the bound that matters is a region's own kernel rate, within a factor of 1–2 of the published speeds. That leaves reductions, synchronisation, attention and hops to fill the rest.
The GPU at batch 1, for comparison A GPU at batch 1 really does use all its bandwidth for one user, through tensor parallelism. One H100 tops out near 3.35 TB/s ÷ 140 GB ≈ 24 tok/s (it cannot even hold the model), eight near 190. The best measured DGX-H100 in HC2024 is 86 tok/s, at "<30% memory bandwidth utilization" Vendor, citing Artificial Analysis. The wafer still wins per user. Each region streams its 1.75 GB of weights in ~6 µs at the measured kernel rate, about 290 TB/s for one layer, so the whole model takes ~0.5 ms. Eight H100s share 26.8 TB/s across all 140 GB, ~5 ms per token Derived.

Speculative decoding on an SRAM machine

Speculative decoding (Leviathan, Kalman and Matias, ICML 2023, arXiv:2211.17192) Indep pairs the big target model with a small, fast draft model:

  1. The draft proposes the next k tokens, one at a time (cheap on a GPU; see below for the wafer).
  2. The target runs once over the context plus those k positions and gets its own distribution at each.
  3. Draft tokens are accepted left to right while they agree (by a rejection-sampling rule that leaves the output distribution exactly the target's). At the first rejection the target's own token is used, and if all k are accepted it adds one more for free.

If each draft token is accepted independently with probability α, the expected number of tokens produced per target pass is

E[tokens per pass] = (1 − α^(k+1)) / (1 − α)          e.g. α = 0.8, k = 4 → 3.36
speedup, GPU (Leviathan et al.)  = E / (1 + k·c)        c = draft cost ÷ target cost per token
speedup, wafer pipeline (here)   = E / (1 + k/S + k·c)  verification pipelined over S stages

On a GPU, step 2 is nearly free: batch-1 decode is bandwidth-bound, so a pass over k + 1 positions reads the weights once and does the extra multiply-adds with otherwise idle compute.

On the wafer that argument fails, and a different one works. The structural reason first. Every weight feeds one multiply-accumulate per position, so a pass over k + 1 positions needs (k + 1)× the multiply-accumulates, and a PE's f16 multiply-accumulate rate (4 measured, 8 at most) is no higher than the rate at which it reads weights. There are no idle FLOPs to hide them in: a region that processed all k + 1 positions together would take ~(k + 1)× the GEMV time Derived. A naive k-wide version of the kernel above agrees, and is worse: 15,028 cycles for k = 2 and 32,110 for k = 4, against 5,648 for k = 1 Measured. (Its extra loop structure makes even k = 1 slower than the table's 5,228, and per-operation set-up dominates, so treat it as an upper bound.) What saves verification is the pipeline. The compiler should not batch the k + 1 positions inside a region; it should feed them one stage behind another, exactly as HC2024's prompt tokens "processed in parallel" do. The first finishes after one full trip and each extra one a single stage-time later. With 80 stages, verifying k = 4 extra positions adds about 4/80 = 5% to the trip Derived. That pipelined verification is what the wafer formula above assumes.

Users in flight

The draft is not cheap on a wafer. On a GPU, c (draft cost ÷ target cost per token) is roughly the ratio of model sizes, because both models are bandwidth-bound: an 8B draft for a 70B target gives c ≈ 0.05–0.1. On the wafer, a draft token is also a trip through a pipeline of regions, and its per-layer collectives, hops and hand-offs do not shrink with model size; they are latency, not bytes. In the budget above, a 70B layer costs ~5,200 cycles of measured GEMV plus ~3,400 of router-speed collectives. A 32-layer 8B draft pays those collectives on every layer as well, so it costs 32 × (3,400 + its own GEMV) cycles against 80 × ~8,700 for the target. That puts c between ~0.15 (a draft spread so thin its GEMV vanishes) and ~0.4 (draft regions sized like the target's); ~0.3–0.4 is the likely range Derived Unverified mapping. Where Cerebras runs its draft, and which model it is, are not published. With c = 0.35, α = 0.8 and k = 4, the per-user speedup is 3.36 ÷ (1 + 0.05 + 1.4) ≈ 1.4×, not the 2.7× that a GPU-style c = 0.05 would give; k = 2–3 does about the same Derived.

What it costs the system, and when it is nearly free. Every position, accepted or not, occupies a stage slot that another user could have used. When the pipe is full, output tokens per slot fall from 1 to E ÷ (k + 1): 67% at α = 0.8, k = 4. But a full pipe needs 80 positions in flight, and the KV arithmetic above says a 4-wafer Llama-70B deployment holds at most ~13 users at 8K context, ~7 once code and task tables are counted. Without speculation those users fill at most 13 of the 80 slots (16%), or ~7 (9%). With k = 4, each user puts 5 positions into the pipe per pass, so the same users occupy at most ~65 or ~35 slots Derived. In this KV-limited regime the slots speculation spends would otherwise sit idle, so it costs the system almost nothing. It costs throughput only when the pipe is full: short contexts, small models, or more wafers per copy of the model. A draft model kept on the same wafers would also take SRAM from the KV cache (an 8B draft is 16 GB at 16-bit), which tightens the user limit further. The KV limit also caps total throughput: ~13 users × 2,100 tok/s ≈ 27,000 tok/s per 4 × CS-3 at 8K context, at most, far below the 600,000 pipe-full ceiling Derived.

Speculative decoding: expected speedup

Expected tokens per target pass E = (1 − αk+1)/(1 − α) (Leviathan et al. 2023). GPU: a pass over k+1 positions costs about one pass, so speedup = E/(1 + k·cGPU). Wafer pipeline: the k extra positions trail by one stage each, so speedup = E/(1 + k/S + k·cwafer). The two draft costs differ because a wafer draft pays latency-bound collectives on every layer. The dashed curve is the share of pipe slots that turn into output tokens, E/(k+1), which is what a full pipe gives up. Where Cerebras runs its draft model, and its α, are not published; c and α here are inputs. All outputs Derived.

What the sources say. Cerebras added speculative decoding in October 2024 and warned that "20% higher or lower than the 2,100 tokens/sec average is normal", which is the signature of a speedup that depends on how predictable the text is Vendor (blog). Mistral Le Chat's "Flash Answers" ran Mistral Large 2 (123B) at "over 1,100 tokens per second", using "speculative decoding techniques developed in collaboration with researchers at Mistral" Vendor (blog, Feb 2025). The Kimi K2.6 deployment credits "custom kernels and speculative decoding" Vendor. No Cerebras source quantifies the speedup from speculation alone, or says which draft model, k or α is used.

Mixture-of-experts, precision and disaggregation

Mixture-of-experts: all resident, few read

In a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model, each layer's feed-forward block is split into many experts, and a router picks a few per token. Kimi K2.6 has ~1T parameters with "32 billion parameters active per token" Vendor (Cerebras blog). Llama 4 Maverick (~400B total, 17B active Vendor, Meta) ran at 2,522 tok/s on Cerebras against 1,038 for a DGX B200 Indep, Artificial Analysis via press release (May 2025). MoE suits pinned SRAM for two reasons. First, capacity is the hard limit, and every expert must be resident, because the router can pick any of them. Second, per-token traffic is low, because only the chosen experts' regions do work: in the budget above, ~32B active parameters cost about the multiply-accumulates of a ~32B dense model. The cost is routing. Tokens must reach the experts' regions and come back, an all-to-all exchange. On GPUs that traffic crosses NVLink. Cerebras says that for Kimi, "all-to-all communications between layers run entirely using on-wafer network fabric" Vendor (May 2026), and that across systems "execution is pipelined to keep high-volume tensor and expert communication within each wafer" Vendor (Hot Chips 2026 blog). A side effect, by the same logic as the pipeline widget: a token occupies only its experts' regions, so the other experts' PEs sit idle unless other users' tokens route to them Derived.

That is a capacity gain, not a speed gain. With 16-bit compute the GEMV is set by multiply-accumulates per cycle, not by bytes: 4-bit weights spread over the same PEs need the same number of multiply-accumulates, and packed onto half the wafers they double each PE's work. So 4-bit buys fewer wafers and more room for KV cache, not a faster token, unless unpacking costs less than the multiply-accumulate it feeds Derived. Computing at 16 bits means unpacking 4-bit weights before the multiply-accumulate. How that is done on the PE is not public. HC2024 listed quantization as a future technique for both "speed" and "footprint" Vendor.

Disaggregation: prefill elsewhere, decode on the wafer

Prefill and decode want different machines. Prefill processes the whole prompt at once, a compute-bound GEMM (Chapter 1). Decode is the latency-bound loop above. Fast decode does not shorten time to first token. CS-4 is marketed with "native support for disaggregated inference": the prompt is processed on another platform (AMD Helios and AWS Trainium are named), and the model state, meaning the KV cache, moves to CS-4 for decode Vendor. In the KV arithmetic above, a single 8K-context Llama-70B user's cache is ~2.7 GB Derived. That is the payload to move per request, and a reason the 2.4 Tb/s CS-4 wafer I/O matters Vendor. At 2.4 Tb/s (300 GB/s) it takes ~9 ms per request, about 19 output tokens' worth of time at 2,100 tok/s, so the transfer has to overlap with other users' decode Derived.

NVIDIA has moved the same way from the other side. After a ~$20B licensing deal with Groq (December 2025), it showed the Groq 3 LPU and LPX rack at GTC 2026 as a decode co-processor next to Vera Rubin NVL72. Each chip has ~500 MB of SRAM at ~150 TB/s, against 22 TB/s of HBM4 per Rubin GPU. Rubin handles prefill and decode attention, and the LPUs run the decode feed-forward and MoE layers; NVIDIA calls this "Attention-FFN disaggregation" (AFD): attention and the feed-forward layers run on different chips Press (Tom's Hardware; ServeTheHome). Most reports, and NVIDIA's localised LPX pages, give the rack as 256 LPUs; one report says 128, which looks like an error (Chapter 17) Vendor via secondary. Supply-chain reports say the Rubin CPX prefill part was set aside Unverified. The comparison is the reticle argument of Chapter 2 again. Many 0.5 GB SRAM chips (a 1T-parameter model at 4-bit needs about a thousand) against 44 GB wafers (about a dozen) Derived.

What sparsity does in practice

The wafer's "native unstructured sparsity" is real hardware. In weight streaming, a zero weight is never sent, so it is never computed: "sparse GEMM is one AXPY per non-zero weight" (Hot Chips 2022, Chapter 8) Vendor. Chapter 3's data-triggered FMAC is the reason it costs nothing to skip. But it was a training claim, and the sparse petaflop headlines (125 PF for CS-3, Chapter 2) assume it. Production inference runs dense 16-bit or native 4-bit weights, as the table above shows. No Cerebras serving announcement claims weight sparsity, and HC2024 lists sparsity only as a future technique Vendor. With pinned weights there is a structural reason too. A sparse weight stored as a 16-bit value plus a 16-bit index (the streamed-weight packet format of Chapter 8, "weight (16b) + index (16b)") takes 4 B per non-zero instead of 2 B. With that format, below 50% sparsity it costs SRAM rather than saving it Derived. The break-even depends on the format: a bitmap of 1 bit per weight breaks even at ~6% sparsity, and block formats lower it further Derived. So storage is not the whole reason. The other is speed: skipping zeros in a resident tile means index-driven gathers, and stepping a DSD one element at a time costs ~33–35 cycles per step (Chapter 6) Measured, against a fraction of a cycle per element for a dense stream. And pruning would change the model's accuracy. So the sparse PF figures say nothing about dense LLM serving.

Check yourself

What does the "match" stage of the compile path do, and why does it make the compiler's job a placement problem rather than a code-generation one?

Match covers the model's graph with kernels from a hand-written library: each group of operations becomes one parameterised spatial program. The code inside each kernel already exists, so what remains is choosing each kernel's size (how many cores, which splits) and location on the wafer, and routing between them: a floorplanning problem.

In the placement problem, why can making one kernel's rectangle bigger fail to speed the network up?

The pipeline runs at the largest Δt over all kernels. Growing a kernel that is not the slowest changes nothing except wire length and area, and it may take area away from the bottleneck. The contest's objective counts maximum compute time, not total.

One user is generating with Llama 70B on four CS-3s. While wafer 3 processes their token, what are wafers 1, 2 and 4 doing?

Nothing for that user: the next token cannot start until this one leaves wafer 4 and loops back. The other wafers stay busy only with other users' tokens in flight. That is why per-user speed and total throughput are separate numbers.

Why can't the 36 GB left on a 4-wafer Llama-70B deployment serve 100 users at 8K context?

Each token of context costs ~0.33 MB of KV cache per sequence, so 100 × 8,192 tokens ≈ 268 GB, far more than 36 GB. At most ~13 users at 8K fit, and only ~7 once each PE's ~4.6 KB of code and task table is counted (Chapter 10), fewer still with buffers Derived.

Roughly how many WSE-3 wafers to hold a 405B model at 16-bit?

810 GB of weights ÷ 44 GB ≈ 19 wafers before any KV cache, or about 5 at 4-bit. In the widget, one 8K-context user (~4 GB of KV) still fits in 19; ten such users need 20 Derived.

Using the measured f16 GEMV (7.5 B/cycle per PE) and 38.9 KB of weights per PE, how long does one layer's GEMV take at 850 MHz, and what does that imply for 2,100 tok/s?

38,900 ÷ 7.5 ≈ 5,190 cycles ≈ 6.1 µs. Eighty layers in sequence give ~490 µs, already more than the 476 µs per token at 2,100 tok/s, before any reduction or hop. But 7.5 B/cycle is this AXPY kernel's limit (~4 f16 multiply-accumulates per cycle), not the hardware's: at WSE-3's 8-wide f16 SIMD it would be ~2,600 cycles, about half the budget. So a higher clock, faster kernels than this naive one (up to ~2×), or more than one accepted token per pass (speculation) must make up the difference Derived.

Why is "four WSE-3s have an 84 PB/s ceiling of ~600,000 tok/s" not a per-user bound?

It assumes all PEs read weights for the same token. In the region mapping, one user's token is in one of 80 layer regions at a time, so it sees ~1/80 of the bandwidth, which gives ~7,500 tok/s at the headline rate (itself an overstatement, since weights can use only the read ports), and ~2,000–4,000 tok/s at a region's GEMV kernel rate (measured vs 8-wide). The 600,000 is a pipe-full total across users Derived.

α = 0.8, k = 4. On a GPU the draft costs c = 0.05; on an 80-stage wafer pipeline it costs c ≈ 0.35. What is the expected speedup on each, why is c so different, and what does the wafer give up when its pipe is full?

E = (1 − 0.8⁵)/0.2 ≈ 3.36. GPU: 3.36/(1 + 0.2) ≈ 2.8×. Wafer: 3.36/(1 + 4/80 + 4 × 0.35) = 3.36/2.45 ≈ 1.4×. (With a GPU-style c = 0.05 it would be 2.7×.) On a GPU both models are bandwidth-bound, so c is about the ratio of their sizes; on the wafer the draft's per-layer collectives and hops are latency that does not shrink with model size. When the pipe is full, each pass uses 5 stage slots for 3.36 output tokens, so system throughput falls to ~67% of the non-speculative rate Derived.

Llama-70B on four CS-3s at 8K context: why does speculation with k = 4 cost the system almost no throughput?

KV capacity limits the deployment to at most ~13 users (~7 after code), so without speculation at most 13 of the 80 pipe slots are busy. With k = 4 each user puts 5 positions in the pipe, at most ~65 slots: slots that would otherwise sit idle. Speculation costs throughput only when the pipe is full Derived.

Why does an MoE model suit pinned SRAM, and what does it cost?

Every expert must be resident (capacity, which SRAM across wafers provides), but only the active ~32B of Kimi's ~1T parameters are streamed per token, so per-token work is like a much smaller dense model. The cost is all-to-all routing of tokens to expert regions, which Cerebras keeps on the wafer fabric, and idle expert regions when few users are in flight.

Why did production inference not use the wafer's unstructured sparsity?

Served models are dense (16-bit originals or native 4-bit). A sparse pinned weight needs an index as well as a value, so it saves SRAM only above ~50% sparsity, and accuracy would change. Sparsity was a training-mode feature of weight streaming, where zeros are simply never sent. (The 50% break-even is for 16-bit indices; a bitmap breaks even near 6%, but skipping zeros in a resident tile still needs slow index-driven gathers.)

Kimi K2.6 is served from its native 4-bit weights with 16-bit compute. Does 4-bit make each token faster on the wafer?

Not by itself. It halves the SRAM per weight, so the model needs about half the wafers and leaves more room for KV cache. But with 16-bit compute the GEMV is set by multiply-accumulates per cycle, and the number of those per token is unchanged; the weights must also be unpacked. 4-bit buys capacity, not per-token speed Derived.

Why does a CS-4 disaggregated deployment, with prefill on another platform, need fast wafer I/O?

The prompt's KV cache has to move to the wafer before decode starts: ~2.7 GB for one 8K-context Llama-70B request. At 2.4 Tb/s (300 GB/s) that is ~9 ms, about 19 output tokens' worth of time at 2,100 tok/s, so it must overlap with other users' decode Derived.
