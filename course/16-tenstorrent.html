Tenstorrent, the middle path

Tenstorrent builds conventional-size chips out of a grid of "Tensix" cores. Each core has about 1.5 MiB of SRAM (1,464 KiB on Wormhole, 1,536 KiB on Blackhole) and five small RISC-V processors that steer a matrix engine. Cheap GDDR6 sits beside the chip. Ethernet ports built into the die (on Wormhole and most Blackhole products) link chips into meshes. Tenstorrent shares the wafer's idea of spatial, explicitly scheduled data movement, but at a very different scale and price.

The chip: a grid on two tori

A Blackhole die is a 17 × 12 grid of tiles (grid locations). There are 140 Tensix tiles, 24 DRAM tiles (each group of three shares one 4 GiB GDDR6 channel), 14 Ethernet tiles, 2 PCIe tiles, 4 L2CPU tiles (each a cluster of four 64-bit SiFive x280 cores that can run Linux), and one ARC and one security tile ISA docs, BlackholeA0 tt-metal SoC descriptor. Every tile has a router on each of two networks-on-chip (NoCs). Each NoC is a 2-D torus: the left and right edges, and the top and bottom edges, are wired together. NoC 0 only flows right and down. NoC 1 connects the same tiles but only flows left and up ISA docs, RoutingPaths.

  • Routing is fixed by the addresses. On NoC 0 a packet travels right, turns at most once, then travels down. On NoC 1 it travels up first, then left. Routers are cut-through ("wormhole switching"). Two packets only contend if they need the same link; crossing inside a router is free ISA docs.
  • Packets and flits. A packet is one header flit plus up to 256 data flits. A Blackhole flit is 64 B, so a packet carries up to 16 KiB; Wormhole flits are 32 B, so its packets carry up to 8 KiB. The NoC clock went from 1 GHz to 1.35 GHz ISA docs, BlackholeA0 NoC.
  • Cost per hop. A router-to-router hop takes 9 cycles, and the NIU (network interface unit) to router step about 5 cycles at each end. Each link carries one flit per cycle ISA docs. That is 64 B × 1.35 GHz ≈ 86 GB/s per link per NoC on Blackhole, against 32 GB/s on Wormhole Derived.
  • Multicast. A write or an atomic can target a rectangle of Tensix tiles; other tile types opt out. The packet follows a tree: along the major axis, then the minor axis, then along the major axis again. Broadcast packets have their own virtual-channel class, so they cannot deadlock against unicasts ISA docs. A virtual channel is a separate set of buffers on the same physical link, so traffic in one channel cannot block another; it is roughly what a Cerebras color gives you on the wafer (Chapter 4).

Inside a Tensix core

Why three cores for one compute kernel? Most of the FLOPs come from the FPU, and the FPU can start only one instruction per cycle. The intended pattern is that one thread feeds the FPU, another keeps the unpackers loading the next operands, and the third keeps the packers draining results. Unpack, math and pack then overlap like stages of a hardware pipeline ISA docs. You still write one compute source file. Metalium compiles it three times, and each binary keeps only the code sections for its own core. The cores are deliberately simple: single-threaded, in-order, and with no caches, "closer to a MIPS R3000 than a phone CPU" METALIUM_GUIDE. A MOP expander and a replay buffer in each frontend turn one pushed instruction into many, so the backend can receive an instruction every cycle without the RISC-V core issuing one every cycle ISA docs.

  • Everything is a 32 × 32 tile. A BF16 tile is 2,048 bytes. In memory it is stored as four 16 × 16 faces, face 0 to face 3, each in row-major order, because the engine works on 16 × 16 faces tensor_layouts report. Tensors are padded up to tile boundaries, so a 48 × 1024 matrix is stored as 64 × 1024 METALIUM_GUIDE. Tilize and untilize run on the device.
  • Registers. SrcA and SrcB each hold 2 banks × 64 rows × 16 columns of 19-bit data. The unpackers fill one bank while the FPU reads the other. Dst holds 1,024 rows × 16 columns of 16-bit data, or 512 rows of 32-bit data ISA docs. That is 16 BF16 tiles. In the default half-sync mode, Math fills one half (8 BF16 tiles, or 4 FP32 tiles) while Pack drains the other matrix_engine; LLM report (Wormhole). The Dst size therefore caps how many output tiles a kernel can accumulate before it packs them.
  • Two engines. The matrix FPU does matmul, reduction and element-wise ops directly on SrcA/SrcB. The 32-lane FP32 SFPU does transcendental and other element-wise functions, and it works on data in Dst METALIUM_GUIDE. An operand smaller than 8 rows wastes the FPU: a 1 × 16 input still costs a full 8 × 16 cycle, so it runs at 1/8 of the rate matrix_engine.
  • Fidelity. The multipliers are 5-bit × 7-bit. LoFi uses the hidden bit plus the top 4 mantissa bits of SrcA and the top 6 of SrcB. HiFi2, HiFi3 and HiFi4 make 2, 3 or 4 passes over different mantissa slices, at 16, 32, 48 or 64 cycles per tile matrix_engine, GEMM_FLOPS. Worked example. A BF16 value has 8 significant bits: the hidden bit plus 7 stored. LoFi multiplies 5 of SrcA's 8 bits by 7 of SrcB's, so it drops SrcA's low 3 bits and SrcB's lowest bit. Split SrcA into a 5-bit and a 3-bit slice and SrcB into a 7-bit and a 1-bit slice, and the full product is 2 × 2 = 4 partial products. Each pass computes one of them and adds it in, so the exact BF16 × BF16 product needs 4 passes, and HiFi4 costs 4 × LoFi's 16 cycles Derived.
  • Block floating point (BlockFP8, BFP). bfloat8_b, bfloat4_b and bfloat2_b store one shared 8-bit exponent per 16 values, rounding to nearest-even after normalising to the shared exponent data_formats report. A bfloat8_b tile is therefore 1,024 B of sign+mantissa plus 64 B of exponents = 1,088 B, about half a BF16 tile Derived. Decode is limited by reading weights, so this halving matters directly.

Glossary for reading Tenstorrent material

NoC, NIU, flit
On-chip network; the network interface unit in each grid tile that turns reads and writes into packets; a flit is the 32 B (WH) or 64 B (BH) unit that crosses one link per cycle.
CB (circular buffer)
A FIFO of fixed-size pages in L1 with producer and consumer counters. There are up to 32 per core on Wormhole and 64 on Blackhole tt-metal circular_buffer_constants.h. Index c_0 is usually the first input and c_16 the output. tt-metal plans to replace CBs with "dataflow buffers".
Unpack / pack
Unpackers copy tiles from L1 into SrcA/SrcB, converting the format on the way. Packers write Dst back to L1 and convert to the output format.
BFP8 / BFP4 (bfloat8_b, bfloat4_b)
Block floating point: 16 values share one 8-bit exponent, so a BFP8 tile is 1,088 B instead of 2,048 B. "BlockFP8" in spec sheets means this format, not GPU FP8 (App A, BlockFP8).
Interleaved / sharded
Interleaved: a tensor's pages are spread round-robin across all banks. Sharded: each core or bank owns one contiguous piece. See the layout section below.
Software stack
TT-Metalium is the low-level C++ kernel and host API. TT-NN is a Python/C++ op library on top of it. TT-Forge and other compilers sit above TT-NN tt-metal README. Most users never write kernels.

Programming: reader, compute, writer

TT-Metalium is an open-source C++ host API in the style of OpenCL. A typical operation runs three kernels per core:

  • a reader on BRISC that pulls tiles from DRAM or from other cores into circular buffers;
  • a compute kernel, compiled into the three TRISC binaries, that consumes input CBs and fills output CBs;
  • a writer on NCRISC that pushes results out.

There is no global thread ID. The host gives each core explicit runtime arguments. The code below is the vector-add example from Tenstorrent's guide, lightly trimmed, plus the single-core matmul compute kernel and the host-side work split METALIUM_GUIDE; tt-metal programming_examples.

reader kernel · runs on BRISC (RISC-V 0), uses NoC 0
void kernel_main() {
    const uint32_t a_addr  = get_arg_val<uint32_t>(0);   // runtime args from the host
    const uint32_t b_addr  = get_arg_val<uint32_t>(1);
    const uint32_t n_tiles = get_arg_val<uint32_t>(2);
    constexpr auto cb_in0 = tt::CBIndex::c_0, cb_in1 = tt::CBIndex::c_1;
    const uint32_t tile_bytes = get_tile_size(cb_in0);
    const auto a = TensorAccessor(TensorAccessorArgs<0>(), a_addr, tile_bytes);  // knows the interleaving
    const auto b = TensorAccessor(/* next args */, b_addr, tile_bytes);

    for (uint32_t i = 0; i < n_tiles; i++) {
        cb_reserve_back(cb_in0, 1);  cb_reserve_back(cb_in1, 1);   // block until a slot is free
        noc_async_read_page(i, a, get_write_ptr(cb_in0));          // DMA tile i from its DRAM bank
        noc_async_read_page(i, b, get_write_ptr(cb_in1));
        noc_async_read_barrier();                                   // wait for both reads to land
        cb_push_back(cb_in0, 1);  cb_push_back(cb_in1, 1);         // hand the tiles to compute
    }
}
compute kernel · one source, compiled for TRISC0 (Unpack), TRISC1 (Math), TRISC2 (Pack)
void kernel_main() {
    const uint32_t n_tiles = get_arg_val<uint32_t>(0);
    constexpr auto cb_in0 = tt::CBIndex::c_0, cb_in1 = tt::CBIndex::c_1, cb_out = tt::CBIndex::c_16;
    constexpr uint32_t dst = 0;
                                                        // runs on:
    compute_kernel_hw_startup(cb_in0, cb_in1, cb_out);   // Unpack, Math, Pack
    add_init(cb_in0, cb_in1, false);                    // Unpack, Math
    for (uint32_t i = 0; i < n_tiles; i++) {
        cb_wait_front(cb_in0, 1); cb_wait_front(cb_in1, 1);  // Unpack: wait for the reader
        tile_regs_acquire();                             // Math: claim half of Dst
        add_tiles(cb_in0, cb_in1, 0, 0, dst);              // Unpack → SrcA/B, Math → Dst
        tile_regs_commit();                              // Math: hand Dst half to Pack
        cb_pop_front(cb_in0, 1); cb_pop_front(cb_in1, 1);    // Unpack: free the input slots
        cb_reserve_back(cb_out, 1);                      // Pack: room in the output CB?
        tile_regs_wait();                                // Pack: wait for Math
        pack_tile(dst, cb_out, 0);                       // Pack: Dst → L1, format convert
        tile_regs_release();                             // Pack: give the Dst half back
        cb_push_back(cb_out, 1);                         // Pack: hand the tile to the writer
    }
}
// SFPU ops need the tile in Dst first:  copy_tile(cb_in0, 0, dst); sin_tile(dst); pack_tile(dst, cb_out, 0);
writer kernel · runs on NCRISC (RISC-V 1), uses NoC 1
void kernel_main() {
    const uint32_t c_addr  = get_arg_val<uint32_t>(0);
    const uint32_t n_tiles = get_arg_val<uint32_t>(1);
    constexpr auto cb_out = tt::CBIndex::c_16;
    const auto c = TensorAccessor(TensorAccessorArgs<0>(), c_addr, get_tile_size(cb_out));
    for (uint32_t i = 0; i < n_tiles; i++) {
        cb_wait_front(cb_out, 1);                          // wait for compute
        noc_async_write_page(i, c, get_read_ptr(cb_out));  // DMA the tile to its DRAM bank
        noc_async_write_barrier();                         // the slot must not be reused before the write leaves
        cb_pop_front(cb_out, 1);                           // free the slot
    }
}

The handshake is the same in every kernel. The producer calls cb_reserve_back, writes, then cb_push_back; the consumer calls cb_wait_front, reads, then cb_pop_front. A slot becomes free only when the consumer pops it, which is after the consumer has finished with the tile. With one slot, the reader therefore cannot fetch tile k+1 while compute is still working on tile k. With two slots it can. That is double buffering, and it is why CBs are usually sized for two tiles METALIUM_GUIDE. On the wafer you never count slots: a CSL program gets the same overlap from the router's hardware input queues and back-pressure (glossary), with depths fixed by the SDK (memcpy.csl gives queues 0–1 / 2–3 / 4–7 depths of 6 / 4 / 2 SDK), and from a CSL FIFO when it needs a deeper buffer in memory.

Circular buffers and double buffering

Reader → CB_in → compute → CB_out → writer, for 8 tiles. A slot is freed only when its consumer pops it. Change the stage times to make a different stage the bottleneck, and turn on DRAM latency spikes to see where deeper buffers start to help.

The compute bar hides a second pipeline of the same kind. Inside it, TRISC0 (unpack), TRISC1 (math) and TRISC2 (pack) overlap on consecutive tiles, handing off through the two halves of Dst (half-sync) rather than through a CB. That inner overlap is why the stepper above ends with all five cores busy at once.

Moving tiles between cores: multicast and semaphores

A NoC transfer is asynchronous. A receiving core cannot tell that data has arrived unless it is told, and Tenstorrent has no data-triggered tasks to do that. Kernels synchronise through semaphores, which are words in L1 updated by NoC atomics. The multicast matmul reader in tt-metal shows the full pattern, simplified here to one operand tt-metal reader_bmm_tile_layout_in0_sender/receiver:

sender (left-column core)
cb_reserve_back(cb_in0, block_tiles);
// read the A block from DRAM
noc_async_read_page(tile_id, s0, l1_addr); ...
noc_async_read_barrier();
// wait until every receiver has said "I have space"
noc_semaphore_wait(sender_sem, num_dests);
noc_semaphore_set(sender_sem, 0);
noc_async_write_multicast(l1_addr,
    get_noc_multicast_addr(x0, y0, x1, y1, l1_addr),
    block_bytes, num_dests);
#ifdef ARCH_BLACKHOLE
noc_async_writes_flushed();  // BH: keep data before flag
#endif
noc_semaphore_set_multicast(recv_sem,
    recv_sem_mcast_addr, num_dests);  // "data is valid"
cb_push_back(cb_in0, block_tiles);
receiver (every other core in the row)
cb_reserve_back(cb_in0, block_tiles);
noc_semaphore_set(recv_sem, INVALID);
// atomic +1 on the sender's semaphore
noc_semaphore_inc(
    get_noc_addr(sx, sy, sender_sem), 1);
// spin until the sender flips our flag
noc_semaphore_wait(recv_sem, VALID);
cb_push_back(cb_in0, block_tiles);
// compute now sees the block in its CB,
// at the same L1 address on every core

The Blackhole-only noc_async_writes_flushed() is there because Blackhole puts the two multicasts in separate command FIFOs, so they could otherwise leave out of order and the flag could arrive before the data tt-metal source comment. Compare this with Cerebras: a wavelet's color selects a route that is configured at compile time (and can be switched at runtime by control wavelets), and its arrival is the signal that starts a task (Chapter 4 and Chapter 5). On Tenstorrent each packet carries its destination address, and "the data is here" is a separate flag that you write yourself.

Anatomy of a matmul

The general-purpose TT-NN matmul ("matmul 2D", MatmulMultiCoreReuseMultiCastProgramConfig) maps the output C = A·B onto a cores_x × cores_y grid tt-metal LLM report §4.3.2:

  • M is split over cores_y and N over cores_x. Each core owns per_core_M = ⌈M/32/cores_y⌉ × per_core_N = ⌈N/32/cores_x⌉ output tiles.
  • K is streamed in blocks of in0_block_w tiles.
  • Outputs are accumulated in sub-blocks of out_subblock_h × out_subblock_w tiles. That product must fit in the Dst half: 8 tiles in BF16, 4 in FP32.
  • The left-column core of each row reads that row's A block from DRAM and multicasts it across the row. The top-row core of each column reads the B block and multicasts it down the column. Each operand therefore leaves DRAM once per row or column, not once per core. The program uses four reader variants (sender or receiver for each operand) and four semaphores matmul_multicore_reuse_mcast. In effect this is a systolic array built in software, and it is the SUMMA dataflow: row broadcast of A, column broadcast of B, local multiply-accumulate. Lab 4 builds the same algorithm on the wafer with <collectives_2d> broadcasts, and measured the fabric at only about 1.5–3% of each step Measured, Lab 4, so there, as here, the inner multiply loop decides how close you get to peak.

The other two LLM variants are matmul 1D, which parallelises over N only and multicasts the width-sharded activation, and the DRAM-sharded matmul used for decode, covered in the next section LLM report.

Where tensors live: interleaved and sharded

A TT-NN tensor is a 2-D buffer of pages. A page is one row (row-major layout) or one 32 × 32 tile (tiled layout). It lives in DRAM or in L1, and its pages are either interleaved or sharded tensor_layouts report.

  • Interleaved. Pages are dealt round-robin over all banks, and every allocation starts at bank 0. In DRAM there are 12 banks on Wormhole and 8 on Blackhole. In L1 there is one bank per core.
  • Sharded. The tensor is cut into height, width or block shards, one per core tensor_sharding report. Sharded activations can stay in a core's L1 from one op to the next with no DRAM round trip. The FlashAttention report calls this "a form of op fusion specific to our framework" FlashAttention report.

One 256 × 256 BF16 tensor, five ways

The tensor is 8 × 8 = 64 tiles. Each colour marks the bank or core that owns a tile.

Why decode keeps its weights DRAM-sharded. In decode, a batch of up to 32 users each contributes one token, so the activations are a single 32-row tile height. The matmuls are bound by how fast they can read weights LLM report. With interleaved weights, every core reads from every bank and the routes cross the whole NoC and collide. The DRAM-sharded matmul instead splits the weights by width across the banks. It places one reader core next to each bank, so each response travels one hop, and gives readers in the same row different virtual channels. On Wormhole this reached 267 of 288 GB/s (92%) in a microbenchmark and 239–260 GB/s (82–90%) in Llama-3-70B and Mixtral decode matmuls vendor-measured, Saturating_DRAM_bandwidth. Try the two presets in the floorplan widget to see the same effect on Blackhole. The decode recipe is: activations width-sharded in L1, weights width-sharded in DRAM, weights in BFP8 or BFP4 LLM report.

Scaling out: from NoC to fabric

Off the chip, Tenstorrent uses standard Ethernet, and the chips themselves act as the switches. TT-Fabric is firmware running on the Ethernet grid tiles' RISC-V cores. It turns NoC reads and writes into packets that are carried to another chip and executed there, so a remote write looks like a NoC write with a longer address (mesh ID, device ID, NoC x/y, offset) TT-Fabric report.

  • Reliability. The link layer adds a header with sequence numbers and resends lost frames (Go-Back-N: after a lost frame the sender rewinds and resends that frame and every frame after it), so the layers above never see CRC errors.
  • Routing. Inside a mesh, packets are source-routed. At each mesh boundary, the entry router writes a new route for the next mesh.
  • Deadlock. Rings and tori use bubble flow control to avoid deadlock: a packet may enter a ring only if at least one buffer slot (the "bubble") stays free afterwards, so the ring can never fill completely and lock up.
  • Routing planes. Wormhole has 4 Ethernet links per direction and Blackhole has 2. Each set forms its own routing plane.
  • Scale. The design targets up to 1,024 meshes of 256 devices TT-Fabric report.

Link line rate is 12.5 GB/s per direction on Wormhole (100 GbE) and 50 GB/s on Blackhole (400 GbE). The measured per-hop latency is 711 ns on Wormhole and 515 ns on Blackhole for the 1-D fabric vendor-measured, CCL bandwidth report. Chip by chip, a Blackhole Ethernet link carries about 1.7× less than one on-chip NoC link (86 GB/s) and about 10× less than the chip's DRAM (512 GB/s); on Wormhole the ratios are about 2.6× (NoC link 32 GB/s) and 23× (DRAM 288 GB/s) Derived. The much larger gap is latency: 515–711 ns per Ethernet hop against one 9-cycle NoC hop, about 7 ns on Blackhole at 1.35 GHz and 9 ns on Wormhole at 1 GHz, so roughly 80× per hop Derived. Every collective between chips pays that latency at each step, which is why how you split a model across chips matters a great deal.

70B on 32 chips

A Wormhole Galaxy ("TG") is an 8 × 4 mesh of 32 chips, with four Ethernet links between neighbouring chips Programming Mesh of Devices report. Collectives can use 4 links along mesh axis 0 and 3 along axis 1 LLM report §3.3.1. On TG, all five Llama matmuls (QKV, dense-out, FF1, FF2, FF3) use 2-D weight fracturing. For FF1 and FF3, each chip holds a [K/4, N/8] block of the weight, and the partial sums are all-reduced across the 4 chips of axis 1. FF2 is the transpose: [K/8, N/4], all-reduced along axis 0 LLM report §2.5.5, §3.3.10. The Llama 3.3 70B Galaxy demo runs in BFP8 llama3_70b_galaxy README. On a torus-cabled Galaxy, its collectives use rings by default. On Blackhole Galaxy, the related demos select a 2-D torus fabric and address the box as the same "TG" mesh llama3_70b_galaxy README.

Back-of-envelope: the decode ceiling Derived

  • Weights: 70B parameters × 1,088/1,024 B per parameter in BFP8 ≈ 74 GB, or 2.3 GB per chip.
  • Wormhole: at 288 GB/s per chip (the DRAM specification, from Saturating_DRAM_bandwidth), one pass over the weights takes 8.1 ms, so the ceiling is about 124 tok/s per user. The measured 72.5 is 58% of that. The rest goes to KV-cache reads, collectives over 12.5 GB/s links and the gaps between ops.
  • Blackhole Galaxy: 16 TB/s / 32 = 512 GB/s per chip, which gives a ceiling of about 220 tok/s per user.
  • BFP4 for some MLP weights raises both ceilings.

This is the memory wall from Chapter 1 again: "decode is bound by time to read model weights from DRAM" LLM report.

Tensix vs the Cerebras PE

Both designs reject the GPU recipe of caches and massive multithreading in favour of explicit, software-scheduled data movement. Tenstorrent's guide compares its design to IBM's CELL. The difference is grain. A Cerebras PE handles a few kilobytes and communicates word by word over routes fixed at compile time. A Tensix core handles about a megabyte and a half, communicates in 2 KiB tiles over addressed packets, and has DRAM behind it.

Why the grains differ: what one message costs. A Tenstorrent packet carries an address, so every transfer pays a header flit and the NIU steps at both ends. Using the hop costs from the NoC section, one 4-byte value sent 5 hops on Blackhole takes 5 + 9 × 5 + 5 + 2 flits ≈ 57 cycles for 4 B. A 2 KiB tile over the same path takes 5 + 45 + 5 + 33 = 88 cycles, about 23 B per cycle Derived. Addressed packets only pay off when the fixed cost is spread over a big payload, so Tenstorrent computes on 2 KiB tiles. On the wafer a color's route is set up before the data moves, so a wavelet has no header: the same 4 B crosses 5 hops in about 10 cycles at 2.0 cycles per hop on WSE-3, and each further wavelet adds about one cycle Measured, Ch 4. The cycles are not the same length (1.35 GHz against the SDK's 850 MHz), but the shape is the point: a static route makes one 32-bit word cheap enough to be the unit of work. The widget runs one decode-style GEMV, y = W·x with BF16 weights, on both machines.

How many PEs is one Tensix? In math per cycle, 4,096 ÷ 16 ≈ 256 PEs at the vendor widths, or about 512 at the measured f16 rate. In memory, 1,536 KiB ÷ 48 KB ≈ 32 PEs Derived. A Tensix packs roughly 8–16× more math per byte of local memory than a PE. That ratio is the grain argument in one number: a Tensix needs DRAM and big tiles to keep its matrix engine fed, while a PE has so little math per byte that its 48 KB can be refilled one wavelet at a time.

Performance and caveats

  • Wormhole Galaxy, Llama 3.3-70B (TP = 32, 128-token input): 72.5 tok/s per user and 2,269 tok/s total at batch 32 (2,269 implies about 70.9 per user; see the footnote under 70B on 32 chips), against a target of 80 tt-metal README table.
  • DeepSeek-R1 671B on 16 Galaxy Blackhole boxes (512 chips), batch 32: ~350 tok/s per user (~300–308 at launch), with ~4 s TTFT Vendor, tenstorrent.com/newsroom/tt-deploy (May 2026); The Register. This is a 512-chip deployment, not one box, so compare it with multi-system results, not with one CS-3.
  • Dispatch matters at this scale. Fast dispatch dedicates one RISC-V core per command queue, usually on an idle Ethernet grid tile. There are two in-order queues, by convention CQ0 for compute and CQ1 for I/O, synchronised with events METALIUM_GUIDE. Ops in decode take microseconds, so tt-metal captures and replays traces to remove the host from the loop. The LLM report puts a whole decode pass at about 10 ms "with microsecond-length op times", too short to wait for a CPU or Linux process scheduling LLM report §4.1. As a rough ratio: if a Llama-70B layer is about a dozen ops (our assumption), 80 layers make about 1,000 ops, so each op averages about 10 µs Derived; a host that needs even a few µs per op to build and send it would add a large fraction to every token. A trace records the command stream once and replays it from device DRAM.
  • Scheduling is your job. In causal FlashAttention, later query chunks do more work. tt-metal gives each core one "low" and one "high" chunk (Q0 with Qn−1, and so on) and measured a 1.6× speedup vendor-measured, FlashAttention report. No hardware scheduler would do that for you.
  • As of November 2026 we found no independent benchmark and no MLPerf submission (none in MLPerf Inference v6.0, April 2026). The 2026 data points are vendor-side: QuietBox 2 (4 × Blackhole, $9,999) runs Llama 3.1-70B at 476.5 tok/s aggregate Vendor, not independently confirmed, and partner Moreh demonstrated MoE inference on Wormhole Galaxy with TT-Deploy without publishing numbers. A 2025 review in The Register described the software as "trapped in a software blackhole" (headline only verified). The firmware de-rating of shipped p150 cards is a reminder that early specs are targets.

Check yourself

Why does one compute kernel need three RISC-V cores, and how do you program them?

The FPU starts one instruction per cycle, so unpack (TRISC0), math (TRISC1) and pack (TRISC2) each get their own in-order instruction stream and overlap like pipeline stages. You write one source file; Metalium compiles it three times and keeps only each core's sections.

Your reader pushes one tile per iteration, but compute calls cb_wait_front(cb, 2) on a CB sized for one tile. What happens?

It hangs. Two tiles can never be in a one-slot buffer, so compute waits forever and the reader blocks in cb_reserve_back. CB size must be at least the largest wait or reserve count, and twice that for double buffering.

A 48 × 1000 BF16 tensor in tiled layout: how many tiles, and how many bytes?

It pads to 64 × 1024: 2 × 32 = 64 tiles × 2,048 B = 128 KiB, against 96,000 B of real data.

On Blackhole, which NoC gives the shorter path from core (3,2) to core (1,5)?

NoC 0 goes right first: 3 → 16 → 0 → 1 is 15 hops with the wrap, then 3 hops down, 18 in all. NoC 1 goes up first: 2 → 1 → 0 → 11 → … → 5 is 9 hops with the wrap, then 2 hops left, 11 in all. NoC 1 wins. Kernels pick a NoC per transfer, so placement matters.

In an 8 × 8 matmul 2D, how many times does each block of A leave DRAM, with and without multicast?

Without multicast, all 8 cores in the row read it: 8 times. With multicast, the left-column core reads it once and sends it across the row.

Where do a 70B model's weights live on a Galaxy, and what does that mean for decode?

In GDDR6. Even in BFP8 they are about 74 GB, against 5.76 GB of SRAM. Decode is therefore capped by DRAM bandwidth, at about 124 tok/s/user on a Wormhole Galaxy. Cerebras inference instead keeps the weights in SRAM, but a 70B model then needs several wafers: four CS-3 systems at launch (Chapter 8). That moves the limit rather than removing it: one user is then bounded at about 2,000–4,000 tok/s by per-region SRAM reads and the latency of 80 layers in sequence (Chapter 9), and the measured ~2,100–2,314 sits inside that range.

A CSL program has a data task bound to a color that adds each arriving wavelet to an accumulator. What is the Metalium equivalent?

A reader that DMAs tiles into a CB, and a compute loop that blocks in cb_wait_front, calls add_tiles (or accumulates in Dst), then pops. Arrival is not a trigger; the kernel polls a counter.
