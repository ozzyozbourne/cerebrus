Chapter 18 · Part IV · Comparison

Head to head

Three answers to the memory wall. NVIDIA keeps a large off-chip memory (HBM) and hides its latency with caches and threads. Tenstorrent gives each core a large SRAM, puts cheap GDDR6 beside the chip, and builds Ethernet into the die. Cerebras removes off-chip memory from the wafer and keeps every byte in SRAM one cycle from its core. This chapter names the axes that decide between them, runs the same workloads through all three, and states plainly what the evidence does and does not show.

After this chapter you can

  • Compare three accelerators on four axes: where the weights live and how fast they are read, compute per byte (the ridge point), how work and data are placed, and cost and power.
  • Work out a decode speed ceiling for each machine and explain why measured Cerebras decode sits far below its bandwidth ceiling while GPUs sit near theirs.
  • Estimate cost and energy per token from stated assumptions, and say which inputs are missing.
  • Say which claims are independently confirmed, and decode the next benchmark headline with a checklist.
  • Pick a platform for a workload and justify the choice from the evidence.

How to compare: the axes

Numbers only mean something along an axis that predicts performance. The course has built three such axes, and a fourth that the evidence covers poorly:

  1. Where the weights live, and how fast you can read them. Capacity times bandwidth of the memory that holds the working set (Chapter 1, Chapter 2). A big slow memory and a small fast one lead to opposite designs.
  2. How much compute per byte. The ridge point = peak FLOP/s ÷ memory bytes/s (Chapter 1). A kernel whose intensity is below the ridge is bandwidth-bound. Decode at batch 1 with 16-bit weights does about 1 FLOP per byte Derived.
  3. How work and data are placed. The programming model: who moves data, and how explicitly (Chapters 10–15 for CSL, 16 and 17 for the other two).
  4. Cost and power. The axis buyers care about most, and the one with the least independent evidence. There is no independent three-way cost or energy-per-token study.

Where does decode sit on each roofline?

One row per device, on a log axis of FLOP per byte of the memory that holds the weights. The shaded part of each row is below that device's ridge, where it is bandwidth-bound. The dot is decode: about 2 FLOP per weight × batch ÷ bytes per weight, ignoring KV-cache reads. Drag the batch size. Hollow ridge marker = the peak is not in the chosen format.

On every HBM or GDDR6 machine, batch-1 decode sits two to three decades left of the ridge, so most of the peak is idle (Chapter 1's table gives the same gaps). Batching moves the dot right because many users share each weight byte. The wafer's ridge is below 1 because its weights sit in SRAM, so on paper decode is not bandwidth-bound there at all. That is the first hint of a result this chapter returns to: measured Cerebras single-user decode is limited by each layer region's own SRAM reads plus pipeline latency and communication, not by off-chip bandwidth.

The trade-off map: capacity vs bandwidth

Log–log. Right = more capacity, up = more bandwidth. Vertical dotted lines are model sizes. Orange diagonals: time to read the whole memory once (1 µs to 100 ms). Tap or focus a point for its value and tag.

The wafer is alone in the top-middle: it can read all 44 GB in about 2 µs Derived. Each DRAM/HBM device takes ~24–60 ms to read its whole memory once (H100 and B200 ~24 ms, p150 ~62 ms) Derived. Of these, only a B200 holds a 16-bit 70B model alone; an H100 holds it only at FP8, and a p150 (32 GB) at no listed precision. Moving right costs height on every machine. A rack buys capacity by adding devices, so its point moves up and right together, along the same diagonal.

The cross-cutting pattern. Two of the three designs answer the memory wall the same way: private SRAM per core, no hardware caches, and data moved explicitly by software between cores. That is why TT-Metalium is the closest relative of CSL. The grain differs. A Tensix core moves 32×32 tiles (2 KB at BF16) in packets up to 16 KiB and always has DRAM behind it. A PE moves single 32-bit wavelets and has nothing behind it but the wafer edge. CUDA is the outlier: it keeps one global address space and lets caches and thread oversubscription absorb latency. These placements explain the evidence below: the wafer wins where the data already sits next to the compute, and the GPU wins where many users can share each byte fetched from HBM.

Scaling

NVIDIA uses NVLink/NVSwitch for scale-up (8 GPUs on HGX H100/B200, 72 in a GB200 NVL72 rack) and InfiniBand or Ethernet for scale-out, with tensor parallelism inside the NVLink domain and pipeline and data parallelism across it (Ch. 17). Tenstorrent uses one Ethernet fabric on the die for both, wired into switchless meshes: cheaper, with lower bandwidth per link than NVLink 5. Cerebras does its "scale-up" on the wafer, which behaves like a rack of chips joined by on-silicon wires. Between wafers it uses MemoryX/SwarmX for training (to 2,048 CS-3s Vendor) and pipelined placement over Ethernet for inference, with switchless Direct Wafer Links arriving in CS-4 (Ch. 8). The comparison of philosophies is a Derived synthesis of Chapters 8, 16 and 17.

The number that predicts each design's scaling is the bandwidth cliff: how much bandwidth drops when data leaves the device.

On the wafer, the bandwidth bound is per region, not per wafer
  • GPUs and Tenstorrent run within a small factor of their bandwidth ceiling: 8 × H100 measured 86 tok/s against a simple ceiling near 190, about 45% (Hot Chips 2024, counting differently, put it at "<30% memory bandwidth utilization" Vendor, HC2024 citing AA), and Wormhole Galaxy 72.5 against ~124, about 58% Derived. More bandwidth there means more speed.
  • Four CS-3s have 4 × 21 PB/s = 84 PB/s, a ceiling near 600,000 tok/s, but that is summed over every user in the pipeline. One user's token is in one of 80 layer regions at a time, so a single user sees about 1/80 of it: ~7,500 tok/s at the headline bandwidth (which also counts writes), and ~2,000–4,000 at real kernel read rates, from the measured f16 matrix-vector kernel to a not-yet-demonstrated 8-wide one (Ch. 9) Derived. The measured 2,314 tok/s Indep, AA via interview sits inside that range: one user's token is a chain of 80 dependent layer reads, plus latency.
  • 1 ÷ 2,314 tok/s ≈ 432 µs per token ≈ 5.4 µs per layer over 80 layers ≈ 4,600 cycles per layer at the SDK's 850 MHz assumption SDK (no WSE-3 clock is published; WSE-2 runs at 1.1 GHz HC22; CS-4 press coverage estimates ~1.4 GHz for WSE-3 Analyst Unverified, which would make it ~7,600 cycles) Derived. That layer's ~1.75 GB sits in about 1/20 of one wafer, ~1 PB/s of headline bandwidth, so reading it takes ~1.7 µs, about a third of the layer's time. At the measured kernel rate (7.5 B per cycle per PE) it takes ~5,200 cycles: about the whole layer budget at 850 MHz, or ~70% of it at ~1.4 GHz Derived. Either way the weight reads are most of the time.
  • So the wafer's single-user pace is set by each region's SRAM reads plus per-layer latency: reductions across PEs, pipeline synchronisation, attention and KV reads, and the hops between wafers. The aggregate bandwidth pays off as throughput across many users. The sources do not break this down (Chapter 9 builds a budget). No per-user H100 or B200 measurement for this model is in the sources.

What actually limits a kernel on the wafer

Put three findings of this course side by side. The ridge widget says decode is above the wafer's ridge, so on paper it is not bandwidth-bound. The decode callout says the pace is set by reads at the measured f16 matrix-vector kernel rate. The GEMM paragraph below says f32 GEMM reaches 32% of peak because of a bank clash and ≈37 cycles per instruction. They are one finding: on the wafer, neither the SRAM nor the fabric is the limit for these kernels; what limits them is what each PE's DSD loop sustains. The decode kernel streams 7.5 B per cycle, about 4 f16 multiply-adds per cycle against the 8-wide f16 SIMD Derived (Ch. 9); GEMM's loop gets 32% of one f32 multiply-add per cycle Measured, Ch. 14; the per-op rates come from Chapter 6 and depend on where the operands sit in the banks. That is the wafer's counterpart of "bandwidth-bound" on a GPU: not "how fast can memory deliver" but "how close does the instruction stream get to one vector op per cycle".

A second workload: a stencil, and a GEMM

The course's own stencil, extrapolated Derived

Lab 3 costs 90 + 2.5·n cycles per step for n cells per PE Measured, Ch. 13. Spread over CS-2's SDK-visible 743 × 992 ≈ 737k PEs at 850 MHz (as Chapter 13's "Scaling up" does; the CS-3 figure is not published, and ~900k PEs would add ~22% to every rate below), n = 16 gives 130 cycles ≈ 0.15 µs per step on 11.8 M cells, about 7.7 × 10¹³ cell-updates/s; n = 5,450 gives about 2.5 × 10¹⁴. An H100 that streams each f32 cell from HBM (4 B read + 4 B write, no temporal blocking) tops out near 3.35 TB/s ÷ 8 B ≈ 4.2 × 10¹¹. The ratio is roughly 180× to 600×. It happens to match the peer-reviewed 179× molecular-dynamics result in size, but that is a coincidence of framing: 180–600× is one wafer against one H100's streaming roofline, while 179× is one wafer against LAMMPS on all of Frontier under strong scaling. Capacity runs the other way: the wafer holds at most ~4 × 10⁹ cells (5,450 per PE), one H100 about 10¹⁰ in 80 GB.

Caveats: a 1-D rod against real 3-D grids; GPU temporal blocking in the 50 MB L2 narrows the gap; and this is a simulator extrapolation, not a hardware measurement.

GEMM is the opposite regime. It is compute-bound, so the ceiling is peak dense FLOP/s: ~15.6 PF FP16 for WSE-3 Analyst against 989 TF BF16 for H100 Vendor, about 16×, for about 33× the power (23 kW system vs 700 W module) Derived. The SDK pattern is benchmarks/gemm-collectives_2d: P steps on a P × P grid with row and column broadcasts SDK, the same structure as Tenstorrent's multicast matmul and a GPU's tiled GEMM. Chapter 14 runs it: on a 4 × 4 grid with 32 × 32 f32 tiles on WSE-3, a step spends 2,569 cycles on the row and column broadcasts and 103,724 on compute, so the fabric is about 2% of the time Measured, Ch. 14. The SDK's loop still reaches only 32% of the one f32 multiply-add per cycle a PE can sustain (operand bank clash plus ≈37 cycles per instruction), and an outer-product rewrite 51% Measured, Ch. 14. On the wafer, f32 GEMM is limited by per-PE instruction efficiency, not by moving tiles.

This is Chapter 2's reticle argument again. The LPX rack cuts an SRAM machine into reticle-sized pieces, so a layer's matrices cross a chip edge every ~0.5 GB, where bandwidth drops ~60×. The wafer crosses an edge only every 44 GB, and the drop there is ~140,000×. So the LPX design must keep many chip-to-chip transfers short and pipelined. The wafer avoids most of those transfers but pays a far steeper price at its edge. Neither side has published per-layer latency breakdowns. The first LPX decode figure is Artificial Analysis's 3,431 output tok/s on Gemma 4 31B at 100K context, as presented by NVIDIA at Hot Chips 2026 Vendor citing AA Unverified (search extract): a 31B model, so not comparable with the 70B rows here. NVIDIA's split also keeps attention and the KV cache in HBM on the GPUs. The wafer has to fit the KV cache in the same 44 GB as the weights.

Cost and energy per token

"GPUs win on throughput per dollar" is the main counterweight to Cerebras's speed. The model below shows why prices differ and what would change them. Every input is editable and tagged; every output is Derived.

A transparent cost and energy model

$ per M tokens = (capex ÷ amortisation hours + kW × $/kWh) ÷ (aggregate tok/s × 3,600 ÷ 10⁶). Joules per token = W ÷ aggregate tok/s. Try raising the Cerebras stream count until the price reaches the $0.60 list price.

With one stream, a 4 × CS-3 instance at the unverified $10M estimate costs about $35 per million tokens, so the published $0.60/M implies roughly 60 concurrent streams per instance, or far lower capex Derived. That is an inference about concurrency that the sources do not report. For H100 there is no capex in the sources; the analyst figure of ~$0.10–0.25/M at large batch Analyst, Spheron is the only GPU data point.

The fair reading Cerebras's independently confirmed advantage is per-user tokens per second and strong scaling (a fixed problem solved faster with more cores) on problems that fit on the wafer. Tenstorrent's case is cost and openness; no independent benchmark shows it beating GPUs on a headline metric. There is no independent three-way cost or energy-per-token study, and no independent measurement of WSE sustained SRAM or fabric bandwidth: every Cerebras bandwidth figure is a vendor number.

How to read the next claim

These numbers will be stale within a year. The checklist will not. Each item has a trap this course has already met:

  1. Dense or sparse? WSE-3's 125 PF is sparse FP16; no dense figure is published. H100's "1,979 TF" is BF16 with 2:4 sparsity (and also its dense FP8 peak).
  2. Which number format? B200's 9 PF is FP4; at BF16 it is about 2.25 PF.
  3. Chip, card, system or rack? 23 kW is a whole CS-3 system; 700 W is one GPU module.
  4. Per user or aggregate? Wormhole Galaxy: 72.5 tok/s per user is ~2,269 in aggregate.
  5. What batch, and is TTFT included? The 72.5 is at batch 32. Fast decode does not shorten prefill.
  6. Quantised? Cerebras said in October 2024 that "all models continue to use 16-bit original weights" Vendor; check each new claim again.
  7. Who measured? gpt-oss-120B: ~3,000 claimed, ~1,700 measured, a factor of 0.57 Derived.
  8. Which spec revision? Rubin HBM4 went from 22 to 19.2 TB/s after announcement.
  9. Shipping or announced? The p150 was cut by firmware from 140 to 120 cores, 774 to 664 TF.
  10. Usable or marketed units? A CS-2's SDK-visible fabric is 750 × 994 ≈ 745k positions against 850k marketed cores; B200 192 GB vs ~180 GB usable.
  11. Is it the paper's number? MD: 179× in the peer-reviewed paper, 748× in a later vendor configuration.

Open questions the sources leave unanswered: how many concurrent streams one Cerebras inference instance serves, and so its true cost per token; tokens per joule on any of the three, measured independently; WSE utilisation on real workloads (CS-1's ~1/3 of peak is the only data point); multi-system training efficiency; why neither Cerebras nor Tenstorrent appears in MLPerf; whether CS-4's "4,400 tok/s" holds up; how many wafers Kimi K2.6 actually uses (~1 TB at FP8 ≈ 23 wafers Derived, unless the deployment does something cleverer; all experts must be resident even though only ~32B parameters are read per token Vendor, via press); what CS-6, which integrates "wafer-scale SRAM and compute with 3D-stacked DRAM" Vendor, does to the capacity limit; and whether Tenstorrent's software closes its gap.

What programming the wafer taught us

Writing and validating five programs for this course made the trade-offs concrete. All numbers are from the SDK 2.10.1 simulator Measured unless tagged otherwise.

  • The hard part is resources and ordering, not arithmetic. The runtime bugs we hit were a race between the host's next copy and the last few data tasks (program 02: the sum after 95 of 100 values; Ch. 7, Ch. 11) and two async fabric operations sharing a microthread (Lab 3: the simulator stops with "trying to term ut_instr[3], but it's not ours" at cycle 2,123 on WSE-3 and 2,125 on WSE-2, and the host then waits forever; Ch. 13). At compile time we met an export declared with the wrong mutability (Ch. 10) and CSL's brace rules for if/else (Ch. 12). The arithmetic was never wrong.
  • Communication is cheap but not free. A Lab 3 step costs about 90 + 2.5·n cycles for n cells per PE. The 2.5 is three vector instructions streaming: 0.5 (@fadds, 2 f32 per cycle on WSE-3) + 1 + 1 (two @fmacs). The ≈ 90 is four one-word transfers, their completion tasks, bookkeeping and instruction start-up. Compute overtakes it only past 90 ÷ 2.5 = 36 cells per PE Derived. In Lab 2 a pure router hop costs 2.0 cycles on WSE-3 and 1.0 on WSE-2, but a hop that goes through a PE's compute (receive, @fadds, send) costs about 9.2 and 6.0 (Ch. 12). Streaming the partial sums beat store-and-forward 101 to 348 cycles at P = 8.
  • Peak rates hide traps. @fmacs runs at half speed when its two source streams start at addresses equal mod 4 words (a bank clash, Ch. 6). The rule is empirical and differs by chip: pad and measure.
  • Weak scaling is flat; capacity is not. Weak scaling means the problem grows with the PEs (n cells each); strong scaling means a fixed problem split over more PEs. Once every interior PE has two neighbours, per-step cost is flat: about 130 cycles at 8 PEs and ~130.8 at 16. At P = 2 both PEs are ends with one neighbour, so a step does two transfers instead of four and costs 101. But each WSE-3 PE leaves only about 44,500 B (≈ 43.5 KB) for arrays (Lab 3 fits about 5,450 cells), and data enters through a ~7 Gb/s-per-channel door SDK.
  • The host dominates small runs. In Lab 2 the kernel is 1–3% of the whole-run cycle count; the rest is host I/O. Its TSC kernel times are 58 / 73 / 101 / 158 cycles for P = 2 / 4 / 8 / 16, about M + 7P for M = 32. Lab 1's @fmacs took ≈ 130 of 7,740 cycles.
  • The simulator is a real asset, with limits. In-kernel timestamp (TSC) readings are stable from run to run, so they pin a per-step cost to within a cycle. Whole-run totals in sim_stats.json are not: they vary by +1–4% with host load, sometimes more, and a loaded run can even abort with "could be a kernel stall". TSC values on different PEs are not comparable, because each counter starts when the launch reaches its PE; time on one PE or use a round trip. A GPU profiler measures real hardware, with run-to-run variance from clocks and scheduling. The simulator trades speed and scale (the largest grid we simulated was 16 × 16 = 256 PEs, in Lab 5) for repeatable cycle counts and a per-instruction trace.

Back to the memory wall

Chapter 1 asked how to feed compute that grows faster than memory bandwidth. NVIDIA buys more HBM bandwidth each generation and raises intensity with batching and fused kernels; the ridge still climbs (295 → ~2,600 FLOP/byte from H100 to Rubin). Since 2026 it also adds small SRAM chips (Groq 3 LPX) for decode, which concedes the wafer's point about bandwidth while keeping the chips reticle-sized. Tenstorrent keeps tiles in a large per-core SRAM, streams weights from cheap GDDR6, and spreads models across chips over Ethernet that costs it almost no bandwidth. Cerebras makes the SRAM big enough to hold the working set and pays in capacity, power per system and communication between PEs. The labs confirmed that answer from the inside: memory was never the bottleneck, messages and their completion tasks were, and every byte had to be placed by hand.

Final check

Why is Cerebras fast at single-user decode, and what limits it?

On a GPU, decode does ~1 FLOP per byte and must stream every weight from HBM per token, so it is bandwidth-bound. On the wafer the weights stay in SRAM spread across ~900k PEs, and each layer region reads its own weights at SRAM speed: about 2,000–4,000 tok/s for one user at real kernel read rates (7,500 at headline bandwidth; the 600,000 ceiling is summed over all users in the pipeline). The measured 2,314 tok/s is inside that range, so the limit is each region's SRAM reads plus per-layer latency (reductions, pipeline synchronisation, hops between wafers), still far faster than streaming weights from HBM.

Why might a provider still serve that model on GPUs?

With large batches GPUs reuse each weight across many requests, reaching a lower cost per token (~$0.10–0.25 vs $0.60 per M tokens, an analyst estimate against a list price). And a single GPU holds 2–4× more memory than one wafer (80–192 GB vs 44 GB), so long contexts and large KV caches fit.

Which of the three programming models is closest to CSL, and how do they differ?

TT-Metalium: both use explicit, software-scheduled data movement between cores with private SRAM and no hardware caches. CSL is finer-grained (32-bit wavelets, not 32 × 32 tiles), routes are declared statically per color, and there is no DRAM behind the PE.

A 400B-parameter model at FP16 must serve 50 tok/s per user to 10,000 users. How many wafers hold the weights, and what does the evidence say?

800 GB ÷ 44 GB ≈ 18.2, so at least 19 wafers before any KV cache Derived. The aggregate demand is 500,000 tok/s. 50 tok/s per user is within a GPU's reach: five B200s (~180 GB usable each) hold the weights, and their 40 TB/s gives a ceiling of exactly 50 tok/s per user, so in practice eight. The deciding question is cost per token, where the only data is an analyst estimate favouring batched GPUs, and the missing data is how many streams one wafer pipeline serves.

With 90 + 2.5·n cycles per step, how many cells per PE before compute is more than half of each step? How would you lower the 90?

2.5·n > 90 at n > 36. The 90 is mostly per-message work (≈ 14.6 cycles per transfer with its completion task, Ch. 13) and instruction start-up, so send fewer, larger messages (a wider halo costs about 1 cycle per extra word) and issue fewer, longer vector instructions. Overlapping the halo with the interior update was measured and made it slower (178 vs 130 cycles), because there was almost no latency to hide.

Which evidence row would you trust least, and why?

Any row with only a vendor tag and no independent check: CS-4's "4,400 tok/s, up to 30×" (unverified), or the gpt-oss-120B claim of ~3,000 tok/s, which independent measurement put at ~1,700. The excluded Tenstorrent blog figure contradicts the vendor's own tables.

Compute the ridge point of an H100 and a B200, and say what it means for decode.

H100: 989 TF ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 295 FLOP/byte. B200: ~2.25 PF ÷ 8 TB/s ≈ 281 at BF16, or 9 PF ÷ 8 TB/s ≈ 1,125 at FP4 Derived. Batch-1 decode is ~1 FLOP/byte at 16-bit (~4 at FP4), so a few hundred times below the ridge: at most ~0.3% of peak. Batching raises intensity for the weights, but each sequence's KV-cache reads stay near 1 FLOP/byte.

Why does Tenstorrent spread a model over 32 chips while Cerebras pipelines layers across wafers?

The bandwidth cliff. A p150's Ethernet (~400 GB/s) is close to its own GDDR6 (512 GB/s), so splitting each layer across many chips costs little. A CS-3's off-wafer I/O (150 GB/s) is about 140,000× below its SRAM bandwidth, so only small activations should cross the edge: whole layers stay on one wafer and tokens move between wafers.
