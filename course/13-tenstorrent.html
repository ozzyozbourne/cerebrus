Tenstorrent, the middle path

Tenstorrent builds conventional-size chips out of a grid of "Tensix" cores, each with 1.4 MiB of SRAM and five small RISC-V processors that orchestrate a matrix engine. Cheap GDDR6 sits beside the chip and Ethernet ports on every die link chips into meshes. It shares the wafer's spatial, explicit-data-movement philosophy, at a very different scale and price.

Inside a Tensix core

  • Everything is a 32 × 32 tile. A BF16 tile is 2,048 bytes; tensors are padded to tile boundaries. The math engine's destination register holds 8 BF16 tiles or 4 FP32 tiles.
  • Two engines. A matrix FPU for tile matmuls and a 32-lane FP32 SFPU vector unit for element-wise and transcendental ops.
  • Block floating point. bfloat8_b and bfloat4_b share an exponent across a block; LoFi/HiFi2/HiFi4 fidelity knobs trade accuracy for throughput.

Programming: reader, compute, writer

TT-Metalium is an open-source, OpenCL-like C++ host API. A typical operation compiles three kernels per core: a reader that pulls tiles from DRAM or other cores over NoC 0 into a circular buffer, a compute kernel (compiled into the three TRISC binaries) that consumes input buffers and produces output buffers, and a writer that pushes results out over NoC 1. Circular buffers (up to 32 per core) synchronise them; sizing one for two tiles gives double buffering. There is no global thread ID: the host hands each core explicit runtime arguments.

Circular buffers and double buffering

Reader → CB_in → compute → CB_out → writer. With a 1-tile buffer, stages wait on each other; with 2 tiles, the reader fetches tile k+1 while compute works on tile k.

Both reject the GPU recipe of caches and massive multithreading in favour of explicit, software-scheduled data movement — Tenstorrent's guide compares its design to IBM's CELL. The difference is grain: a Cerebras PE handles a few kilobytes and communicates word by word; a Tensix core handles megabytes and communicates tile by tile, with DRAM behind it.

Performance and caveats

  • Wormhole Galaxy, Llama 3.3-70B: 72.5 tok/s per user (~2,269 tok/s total at batch 32) Vendor repo table.
  • Galaxy Blackhole, DeepSeek-R1 671B: ~350 tok/s per user (~308 at launch) Vendor.
  • No independent benchmark or MLPerf submission was found. A review described the software as "trapped in a software blackhole" (The Register, 2025). The firmware de-rating of shipped p150 cards is a reminder that early specs are targets.
  • The tt-metal LLM report says decode is "bound by time to read model weights from DRAM" — the same memory wall as Chapter 1, met with ~512 GB/s per card.

Check yourself

What do BRISC and NCRISC do, and what do the TRISCs do?

BRISC and NCRISC issue NoC data movement (reader/writer); TRISC0/1/2 issue unpack, math and pack instructions to the Tensix coprocessor.

Why size a circular buffer for two tiles?

So the producer can fill one slot while the consumer works on the other — double buffering hides transfer time behind compute.

Where would a 70B model's weights live on a Galaxy, and why does that matter for decode?

Mostly in GDDR6 (6.2 GB SRAM vs 140 GB of weights), so decode is limited by GDDR bandwidth, unlike a Cerebras deployment that pins weights in SRAM.
