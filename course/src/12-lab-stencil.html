Lab 3: a heat stencil, measured

The capstone program simulates heat flowing along a rod split across a row of PEs. It needs everything from Part II: two-way neighbour traffic on four colors, asynchronous transfers, completion tasks counted into a step, and an iterative state machine. Then we measure what each timestep really costs inside the simulator.

After this lab you can

  • Decompose a grid over PEs with ghost cells and a halo exchange.
  • Drive an iterative algorithm entirely from tasks.
  • Measure kernel cost in the simulator by differencing runs, and fit a simple performance model.

The problem

Explicit finite differences for the 1-D heat equation: every timestep, each cell moves toward the average of its neighbours,

u'[i] = u[i] + α · (u[i-1] − 2·u[i] + u[i+1])        α = 0.25, ends held at 1.0 and 0.0

The rod has P × n cells. PE k owns cells k·n … k·n+n−1 and stores them in u[1..n], with ghost cells u[0] and u[n+1] holding copies of its neighbours' edge cells. Each step, every PE sends its first cell west and its last cell east, receives the neighbours' edge cells into its ghosts, then updates.

The hang The first version used output queues 2/3 and input queues 2/3. It compiled, then stalled with no progress at cycle 1,894 and had to be killed. On WSE-3, async fabric operations run on the microthread with their queue's ID, so the east send and the west receive both claimed microthread 2. Moving the input queues to 4/5 made all four operations independent. The comment in pe_program.csl records why. Rule: give every concurrently active fabric operation its own queue number.

What the numbers say

  • A fixed ~88 cycles per step, plus ~2.5 cycles per cell. The three points fit cycles/step ≈ 88 + 2.5·n exactly (n = 4 → 98, 16 → 128, 64 → 248). The 2.5 cycles per cell covers three vector instructions, under one cycle per element per instruction. The fixed part is the halo exchange — four single-wavelet async transfers, their completion tasks, and task scheduling.
  • Weak scaling is flat. 8 PEs and 16 PEs cost the same per step. Every PE only talks to its neighbours, so adding PEs adds no work to anyone. This is the property that lets a stencil use all ~900k PEs.
  • P = 2 is cheaper because both PEs are edge PEs with only one neighbour: two transfers per step instead of four.
  • WSE-2 vs WSE-3 is a wash for this kernel (125 vs 128). f32 work does not benefit from WSE-3's wider FP16 SIMD.

Use the model

Extrapolate 88 + 2.5·n cycles per step. The 850 MHz clock is the SDK benchmarks' own assumption; no official clock is published.

The lesson is surface-to-volume. With 16 cells per PE, two-thirds of each step is communication and synchronisation. With thousands of cells per PE (memory allows about 5,000 f32 cells plus the scratch array), compute dominates. On a real wafer you would also go to 2-D domains and overlap the halo exchange with the interior update — the SDK's 7-point and 25-point stencil benchmarks do both.

Exercises

  1. Overlap communication with compute: update the interior cells u[2..n-1] while the halo transfers are in flight, and only the two edge cells after. Re-measure: does the fixed 88 shrink?
  2. Run 1,000 steps and confirm the profile approaches the straight line between the two boundary temperatures.
  3. Switch the arrays to f16 and the builtins to their half-precision forms. Compare WSE-2 and WSE-3 cycles per step.
